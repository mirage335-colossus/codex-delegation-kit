# EME near 5.8 GHz: physical and measurement basis

## Decision handoff

Treat an Earth–Moon–Earth link as a **bistatic, distributed, polarization-sensitive passive-scattering path**, even when the same station transmits and receives. A monostatic radar equation is a valid special case only after transmitter and receiver ranges, gains, pointing and polarization are made equal. The Moon is not a point mirror or a uniformly illuminated hard sphere: a strong near-subradar quasi-specular component coexists with weaker distributed returns, whose geometry creates a long delay tail, libration/rotation-dependent differential Doppler, and coherent fading. Antenna beam patterns select the illuminated/received lunar patch; the scalar lunar radar cross section must refer to that patch, carrier, incidence and polarization.

For a receiver, separate (1) the dominant common Doppler/range trend of the Earth–Moon–Earth path, (2) residual Doppler spread across scattering points, (3) amplitude fluctuations/fades within that distributed response, and (4) terrestrial interference and front-end limits. The 7.209 GHz X-band study derived footprint-dependent widths from its DE436 geometry: 35 Hz for a 503 km receive footprint and 90 Hz for a 1,118 km footprint. The paper did detect the echo, but those widths are geometry-derived, not direct measurements of an observed spectral envelope. A same-footprint first-order conversion to 5.8 GHz gives about 28 Hz and 73 Hz, but this is a conditional scaling, not a measured 5.8 GHz width or a universal operating range.

There is enough direct operational evidence to say 5.76 GHz EME is feasible with practical amateur stations: a 2024 station log records multiple contacts using 37 W and a 3 m dish, and a separate 2008 contest entry documents 5.76 GHz EME on a 5 m dish with 70 W at the feed. The latter reports 802.11a neighborhood interference as a practical problem. These are not calibrated link-budget campaigns. Missing the intended receiver dish/feed efficiency, transmitter power reference plane, polarization, pre-LNA loss, noise temperature, antenna pattern/pointing, target SNR statistic, and coding/integration settings prevents an absolute 5.8 GHz product-feasibility claim.

A reproducible source-matched check is in `link_budget.py`. For the 2020 paper's 7.209 GHz bistatic setup, a conventional bistatic radar equation and the paper's *own* noise-power-standard-deviation SNR definition give 177,688 (52.50 dB) versus the paper's reported ~167,000. This close result validates the units, reference planes and estimator convention in the calculation. The unintegrated `Pr/kTB` is 39.78 dB for the same example and must not be compared directly to the paper's integrated SNR. A separate lunar mapping archive reports absolute backscatter estimates 6–10 times lower than previous investigators despite inter-run consistency. That factor alone corresponds to 7.8–10 dB in received power and overwhelms small refinements to a nominal budget.

The permitted current `pumpModem/docs/channel-models.md` describes an existing native Robust sampled `StreamingChannel` with a two-path family, selected excess-delay support, differential Doppler and a separate common-frequency excursion. This is a sensible implementation base for reusing the native continuous channel and receiver path when adding explicitly synthetic EME capture/stress cases. The same document cautions that this two-path family does not cover arbitrary diffuse scattering or independent carrier fades, and its currently named `HF Worst` bounds are HF simulation choices, not measured lunar inputs. Therefore retain the existing generic path/test machinery, but do not call its HF preset an EME channel or treat its realization as measured lunar behavior. An EME extension should source its center drift and surface-supported delay/Doppler from stated geometry, keep common and differential effects separate, and retain a dominant component plus a diffuse/libration-dependent tail only if receiver decisions require that extra structure.

## Physical channel and observables

### 1. Geometry and path reference planes

For a scattering point `x` on the lunar surface and terrestrial transmitter and receiver sites `T` and `R`, define the signal geometry at the reflection event by

`L(x,t) = R_T(x,t) + R_R(x,t)`

with `R_T = |x - T|`, `R_R = |x - R|`. The two-way bistatic excess delay relative to a chosen reference point `x0` is

`Δτ(x) = [L(x)-L(x0)]/c`.

The nominal signal delay is `L(x0)/c` (about 2.56 s at mean geocentric range for a same-site monostatic path). The predicted carrier offset follows the derivative of the total path, with sign determined by the receiver's convention; to first order its magnitude is `f0 |dL/dt|/c`. For a colocated radar, the two legs are nearly equal and the monostatic two-way Doppler term is about twice the one-leg radial velocity. For separated EME stations, use each site and each leg. Approximation by twice one geocentric range-rate misses station-dependent topocentric terms, reflection epoch, libration and the unequal receive/transmit paths. The exact estimator should compute/track the expected carrier at the selected reference scattering point and then represent the surface-dependent residual around it.

Reference planes matter. In the bistatic radar equation, `Pt` is power delivered to the transmit antenna reference plane, `Gt` is directional antenna gain toward the Moon, and `Gr λ²/(4π) = Ar,e` is effective receive aperture. If the measured power is before a feed loss, after a receive filter/LNA, or referenced to a demodulator output, the corresponding losses/gains/noise bandwidth must be carried consistently. `RCS σ` is not a universal property independent of direction: it depends on frequency, polarization, incidence and the scattering model. For the Moon it must also be spatially and beam weighted if the footprint is finite.

For a compact idealized target, the receive power is

`Pr = Pt Gt Gr λ² σ / [(4π)^3 R_T² R_R²]`.

Equivalently, using receive effective aperture `Ar,e`,

`Pr = Pt Gt σ Ar,e / [(4π)^2 R_T² R_R²]`.

For `R_T = R_R = R` and shared transmit/receive gain, this reduces to the monostatic form `Pt G² λ² σ/[(4π)^3 R⁴]`. For an extended surface, the physical operation is an integral over surface elements, not substitution of the full lunar disk's area into a point-target formula:

`Pr ∝ ∫_Moon Pt Gt(θT,x) Gr(θR,x) λ² σ0(x; f, pol, incidence) / [(4π)^3 R_T(x)² R_R(x)²] dA`.

The return waveform applies the point-dependent delay and Doppler before coherent summation. This matters at 5.8 GHz because a few-degree reflector/feed error, a beam footprint that covers only part of the Moon, or a polarization mismatch changes both which lunar elements contribute and how their complex returns add. A single scalar echo power is therefore an observable after an explicit time/frequency/polarization integration, not a freestanding channel truth.

### 2. Scattering, delay, and distributed Doppler

Short-pulse lunar radar observations contradicted the old uniformly bright rough-disk assumption. Evans and Hagfors describe a quasi-smooth body: the leading edge is associated with the nearest portion and often has a few narrow, highly correlated spikes; farther into the echo, the illuminated region grows, correlations fall and the aggregate becomes more random. This is a useful physical decomposition for communication: a strong leading/specular-like lobe plus weaker spatially distributed components. It is not a guarantee of an isolated clean path. Some early work at 488 MHz inferred the lunar scattering character from two orthogonal polarization channels and identified two fade mechanisms: faster, seconds-scale libration-related fades and slower, 20–30 minute ionospheric Faraday-rotation fades. Faraday effects do not scale to 5.8 GHz as they do at VHF; the older timing is evidence for the mechanism in its observed band, not an expected 5.8 GHz fade period.

The visible hemisphere is physically extended in delay. For a spherical Moon of radius 1,737.4 km, the exact difference in two-leg propagation time between the near subradar point and geometric limb is about 11.564 ms. This is a geometric support bound for the visible spherical surface, not a prediction that echo power is uniformly present across 11.6 ms. The early leading/near-center component can dominate; actual energy-versus-delay depends on scattering law, range resolution and antenna weighting. The 1974 radar-mapping procedure used pulse intervals that avoid echo ambiguity, continuously adjusted center delay and Doppler, and selected observation time using lunar libration and position angle. It also warns that a given delay-Doppler cell maps to ambiguous surface locations unless beam geometry/observing conditions separate them. Delay and Doppler alone do not always identify a unique patch.

Lunar rotation/libration assigns each scatterer a radial velocity. The 2019 7.209125 GHz bistatic experiment used the DE436 lunar model and evaluated 49 surface locations around its target. It derived echo frequency ranges of 14,596–14,631 Hz for a 503 km footprint and 6,608–6,698 Hz for a 1,118 km footprint: geometric spans of 35 and 90 Hz, respectively. These widths differ because the receiving beam footprints differ. The paper separately reports measured echo frequency deviations of −15 Hz and −10 Hz from prediction at two receivers, possibly due to beam-center coordinate error. The central carrier shift is not the same quantity as the within-footprint span. At fixed geometry and target footprint, Doppler spread scales approximately linearly with carrier: the 35 Hz at 7.209 GHz maps to 28.2 Hz at 5.8 GHz; the 90 Hz case maps to 72.4 Hz. These are scale checks only. A different lunar aspect, TX/RX baseline, beam taper or incidence-weighted scattering distribution can alter the spectral shape and width.

A practical model should distinguish:

- **Common path trend**: slowly varying mean delay and carrier Doppler for a selected surface/reference point, driven mainly by the changing Earth–Moon geometry and site motion. This calls for delay/frequency wipeoff or a search region around an ephemeris prediction.
- **Distributed differential path**: residual delay and Doppler of each lunar patch relative to the chosen reference point, arising from lunar radius, shape, rotation/libration, changing station geometry and the finite beam footprint.
- **Complex fading**: echoes from separate scattering areas add with phase; a changing aspect can change phasor sum and polarization. A fixed independent Gaussian noise sample or a single deterministic attenuation cannot represent all deep fades.
- **External impairment**: local 5.76 GHz interference, atmospheric attenuation, receiver blocking, tracking loss and calibration error are separate causes and should not be relabeled as lunar Doppler/fading.

The 1974 coordinate treatment makes another important point: delay-Doppler resolution cells are finite and often oblique on the surface; mapping depends on a Jacobian and geometry, while increased coherent integration improves Doppler resolution but reduces the number of independent samples for average-power precision. Therefore, a receiver's bandwidth/SNR statistic and integration interval belong beside the signal width; “SNR” without a noise estimator, bandwidth and integration convention is ambiguous.

### 3. Polarization and receiver noise

The 7.209 GHz bistatic experiment transmitted left-hand circular polarization and simultaneously received same-sense and opposite-sense circular channels. It used a radar albedo of 0.07 for the opposite-sense channel in its SNR estimate. This polarization split is scientifically informative: smooth-sphere reflection reverses circular sense; roughness and multiple scattering return components in both channels. Lunar depolarization is real, but a standard two-channel radar's SCP/OCP ratio is not the same thing as an EME station's net antenna mismatch loss. Do not substitute one for the other without matching transmit and receive basis, convention, surface footprint and channel calibration.

Operational reports give a useful practical counterpoint. In 2017, a successful 10.368 GHz QRA64D QSO linked a station using 50 W into a 1.13 m linear-polarized dish and another using 150 W into a 2.2 m circular-polarized dish. The authors estimated that changing from linear to circular cost somewhat less than the ideal 3 dB, about 2 dB, because the Moon depolarizes. This is one successful configuration, not a general polarization law. The 1296 MHz system dissertation separately identifies spatial basis rotation between Earth stations, ionospheric Faraday rotation and nonideal feed/antenna polarization as contributors to mismatch, and favors circular polarization for robustness. At 5.8 GHz ionospheric Faraday rotation is strongly less consequential than at VHF, while surface depolarization, feed axial ratio/orientation and station agreement remain.

For received noise, use the antenna-convolved brightness, not a fixed “space is 2.7 K” assumption. ITU-R P.372-15 states the Moon brightness above 1 GHz is nearly frequency independent and varies from about 140 K at new moon to 280 K at full moon. A 5.76 GHz station report directly measured more than 2 dB of Moon noise during a 2016 pass. The observed antenna-temperature rise depends on Moon phase, dish beam pattern, pointing, sidelobes/spillover and `T_rx`; it is not simply 140–280 K added at the LNA input. For a beam larger than the apparent disk, beam coupling dilutes this brightness; for a narrow beam centered on the disk, it approaches lunar brightness in the main-beam contribution. Keep at least `T_Moon,beam`, sky/CMB, atmosphere, ground spill, antenna/feed loss and receiver input noise distinct. The same high gain and accurate pointing that raise signal collection can raise captured lunar noise and expose nearby RFI.

### 4. Gain, pointing, surface, atmosphere and feasibility

A reflector's gain follows `G = 4π η_ap A_phys / λ²`, and its effective area is `η_ap A_phys`. In a point-target monostatic budget, a 1 dB gain loss shared on transmit and receive is about a 2 dB echo-power loss; in a bistatic link, transmit and receive gains enter once each. The 1296 MHz technical dissertation emphasizes optimizing `G/Tsys`, not peak gain alone: under-illuminating a dish can lower spillover/noise even while reducing gain. It also notes depointing affects a monostatic echo twice and recommends accurate rotor calibration and celestial-source beam-pattern measurements. For EME the Moon is about 0.5° across, so a beam comparable with that angular size requires integrating gain over the disk/footprint; pointing offset may reduce total coupling or change which surface region dominates. Feed misplacement, efficiency, reflector mesh, spillover, rotor backlash and pre-LNA feed loss all change the measured SNR and must be measured at the actual station rather than assumed from dish diameter.

Atmosphere acts on both legs, and weather and elevation may differ at the two ends of a bistatic QSO. ITU-R P.676 supplies gaseous attenuation methods from 1 to 350 GHz, while P.838-3 specifies specific rain attenuation `γ_R = k R^α` for frequency, rain rate and polarization. As a reproducible conditional example, the script interpolates the P.838-3 5.5/6 GHz coefficients to 5.8 GHz, assumes circular polarization, 25 or 100 mm/h rain at *both* sites, a uniform 1 km vertical rain layer at each station, and 30° elevation (2 km slant rain path at each endpoint). It estimates 0.34 dB combined two-end attenuation at 25 mm/h and 3.15 dB at 100 mm/h. This is a deliberately transparent sensitivity, not a rain climatology or availability probability; actual rain-cell depth/extent, site weather and wet antenna/feed losses are omitted. A false cancellation would assign one endpoint's loss `+0.17 dB` and the other's `−0.17 dB`, yielding 0 dB; physical one-way losses are both negative and add to −0.34 dB. At 100 mm/h the same wrong cancellation hides 3.15 dB. No 5.8 GHz gaseous-loss number is calculated because site/elevation/climate inputs are missing; keep gas, rain-path loss and wet feed/reflector loss separate.

At 5.760 GHz practical amateur EME is not merely theoretical. A December 2024 report documents 37 W at a 3 m dish and multiple worked stations during a Moonpass event. An ARRL 2008 contest report documents a 5 m dish and 70 W at the feed, and says local 802.11a overrun made the band challenging and motivated changing frequency. These operational records establish feasibility and interference sensitivity, but omit complete receiver `G/T`, waveform SNR definition, per-QSO moon distance, elevation, cross-polarization, weather and pointing. They cannot validate a universal minimum dish/power threshold. At 10.368 GHz, the 2017 record establishes successful low-power/small-dish contacts and a practical cross-polarization observation, but has a different carrier, stations, geometry and coding.

For an antenna-scale check only, `link_budget.py` uses a 0.55 aperture-efficiency assumption at 5.760 GHz with reported 3 m and 5 m dish diameters. It calculates approximate 42.6/47.0 dBi gains and 1.21°/0.73° HPBW, respectively, using the conventional `70 λ/D` beamwidth approximation. A Gaussian *point-source* boresight model gives 0.51 dB loss at a 0.25° offset for the 3 m dish and 1.42 dB for the 5 m dish. The Moon is a 0.5° extended source, so these are not full-disk coupling losses; they establish why the actual antenna pattern and beam/Moon overlap are needed rather than an arbitrary fixed pointing loss.

The 2020 X-band bistatic radar setup is observatory-scale: 200 W at an 18 m transmit dish, then 40 m (η=.47, 96 K system temperature) and 13.2 m (η=.7, 40 K) receive dishes. It confidently detected the echo for a 60-minute session at 7.209125 GHz. These parameters are strong evidence that X-band lunar scattering can be measured and that `G/T`, footprint and coherent integration control feasibility; they are not a plausible unqualified consumer modem configuration. For realistic 5.8 GHz communication, the actual transmitter EIRP, two station gains, noise temperatures, code/detector bandwidth and duty cycle are required.

### 5. Calculation details and quantitative interpretation

`link_budget.py` reproduces one published 7.209 GHz receive-power/SNR case and computes conditional geometry/frequency scalings. Inputs are explicitly taken from the 2019-observation paper: Tx 200 W, 18 m reflector at η=.50; Rx 40 m reflector at η=.47 and `Tsys=96 K`; center range 384,400 km; 503 km projected receive footprint; opposite-sense radar albedo 0.07; 35 Hz echo bandwidth; 10 s integration. It uses `λ=c/f`, `A_e=ηπD²/4`, `G=4πA_e/λ²`, `σ=0.07π(503 km/2)²`, and the bistatic radar equation. The paper's SNR definition is `Pr/ΔPn`, where `ΔPn=k Tsys B/√(B Δτ)`. This yields `Pr=4.406×10⁻¹⁶ W = −123.56 dBm`, instantaneous `kT B=−163.34 dBm`, and integrated metric 177,688 (52.50 dB), close to its reported 167,000 (52.23 dB). The small remaining difference is consistent with rounding and simplified footprint/range; the study did not provide a station execution or independently extract raw data, so this is a reference calculation, not a new observation.

The calibrated lunar 12.6 cm PDS dataset reports its dimensionless calibrated SCP/OCP backscatter values are consistent over many observation periods, but lower by factor 6–10 than prior investigators for unknown reasons; the relative channel ratios are better calibrated. Using that factor as a **sensitivity**, not a probability distribution or 5.8 GHz measured correction, lowers the source-matched predicted echo by 7.78–10.00 dB. This proves why sigma convention/absolute calibration should be prioritized over a nominal “average lunar RCS” point estimate.

Other script outputs are geometry checks: 2.564441 s mean-range roundtrip; 11.564 ms front-surface subradar-to-limb delay range using the exact finite-distance tangent-limb range; perigee/apogee range sensitivities of +1.31/−0.98 dB under `R⁻⁴` scaling; and a 5.8 GHz width scaling of 28.16 Hz from the 35 Hz, 7.209 GHz geometry-derived footprint span. The plane-wave `2R_Moon/c` approximation is 11.591 ms; its 0.027 ms difference is only the finite-distance tangent geometry correction, not a disagreement in lunar propagation support. The script converts the 35/90 Hz spans to equivalent total path-rate spans of 1.455/3.743 m/s. This uses `Δf ≈ f0 Δ(dL/dt)/c`; at 5.8 GHz, each 1 m/s of total path-rate span corresponds to 19.35 Hz. The path-rate conversion makes the distinction concrete: the tens-of-hertz spread is a small residual about a much larger predicted carrier shift and should not be folded into the common Doppler estimate.

The script's 5.8 vs 7.209 GHz frequency-only budget factor (−1.89 dB for fixed physical apertures and identical sigma) is exactly the antenna-gain/wavelength term for fixed apertures; it explicitly does **not** extrapolate lunar RCS or atmospheric loss. A fixed dish gain at a different frequency, different efficiencies, local 5.8 GHz RFI, weather and actual antenna pattern can reverse or overwhelm this small factor. The number is only useful to stop a common mistake of using identical `Gt/Gr` and identical `λ²` independently without noticing that dish gain itself changes with λ.

## Competing modeling choices and failure regimes

1. **Point reflector / free-space Friis only** — cheap and useful for nominal EIRP/gain/range accounting, but it hides distributed delay-Doppler shape, surface polarization, point-dependent interference and beam-weighted RCS. It fails for waveform capture, spread/fade margins, or sub-disk illumination. Retain as a scalar upper-level link-budget approximation only with explicit sigma and detector convention.
2. **Uniform full-disk diffuse scatter** — can provide a conservative first support bound and a simple integrated-power estimate when beam is broad, but contradicts measured leading spikes and strong central specular returns. It overstates early-delay energy and can misstate polarization and cross section. Reject as the only model; it may be a diffuse-tail component or a stress envelope.
3. **Quasi-specular model (Hagfors-type angular law)** — explains the concentrated return and historical 3.8/23 cm backscatter, but fitted `C` is not uniquely an RMS slope; the interpretation depends on a spatial cutoff and changed subradar point/libration. A single fitted law cannot stand for local maria, highlands, incidence, or 5.8 GHz polarization without validation. Reuse as a parameterized dominant component with its fitting/scale convention visible.
4. **Specular plus diffuse composite** — best matches dual-polarization, wavelength-dependent evidence: quasi-specular surface/interface and diffuse wavelength-scale rocks/regolith contributions. It supports an early dominant component plus a distributed tail, but the weights vary by terrain, incidence and wavelength; available datasets at 3.8, 12.6, 23 and 70 cm do not directly produce an exact 5.8 GHz link-wide law. This is the recommended science structure if the receiver needs more than scalar echo power.
5. **Deterministic physical libration/DE ephemeris** — required to center the search and predict event geometry/center Doppler. It does not determine sigma, phasor amplitudes, system temperature or RFI, and errors in epoch/reference point can look like signal frequency error. Use exact bistatic retarded geometry where engineering design needs precision; keep an explicit residual acquisition margin.
6. **Statistical fading-only model** — can stress detector availability but cannot preserve path-common sweep, deterministic delay support, seasonal lunar geometry or measured interference. Do not assign an unmeasured Rayleigh/Rician process as empirical truth. Use controlled synthetic fading only as an explicitly synthetic stress after the deterministic reference path and measured spectra are in place.

Credible hard failure conditions are: local 5.8 GHz interferers/receiver compression or desense (explicitly seen in a 5.76 GHz contest station); inadequate dish gain/pointing or TX/RX beam overlap; wrong Doppler sign/reference/retardation causing the narrow search to miss the echo; cross-polarization or feed/rotator mismatch; using a full-disk RCS with a narrow actual beam or the converse; weather/feed water loss at low elevation; Moon-noise rise reducing `G/T`; and lunar deep fades caused by coherent scatterer cancellation. Include these separately so a failed link has a diagnosable cause rather than an inflated undifferentiated path-loss margin.

## Remaining engineering dependencies

Before using an absolute 5.8 GHz EME SNR or availability claim, request: both stations' `Pt` at the antenna reference plane, antenna diameter/efficiency/pattern and tracking/pointing error, feedline/pre-LNA losses, polarization basis/axial ratio, measured receive `Tsys`/`G/T`, waveform occupied bandwidth and integration/SNR statistic, desired delay-Doppler capture range, local interference spectrum/front-end headroom, and station weather/elevation assumptions. For a full waveform model also state whether communication uses two geographically separated stations or a monostatic echo test. These inputs determine which geometry is relevant and which measurements can discriminate the model.
