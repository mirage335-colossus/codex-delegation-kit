# Noise, interference, and receiver reference planes

## Decision handoff

**Recommendation:** retain the native receiver's current sampled noise and impairment hooks for controlled algorithm tests, but treat them as synthetic channel-space stresses, not environmental noise or RF-front-end qualification. Add a source-separated, calibrated input model only after fixing a physical reference plane and collecting a target chain's noise and blocker measurements. Keep the measured amplitude distribution and event timing when the noise is not demonstrably complex Gaussian. **Most important conditions:** 1 MHz external noise is commonly dominated by atmospheric and man-made sources, but quiet-site/global median curves are not valid site guarantees. ## Measurement meanings and reference planes

ITU-R P.372-17 (§1.2) defines external noise factor (f_a=p_n/(kT_0b)), with (p_n) the available noise from an equivalent **lossless reference antenna**, (T_0=290) K and (b) the receiving-system noise-power bandwidth. (F_a=10\log_{10}f_a) is external-noise figure relative to (kT_0b); equivalently (f_a=T_a/T_0). It distinguishes antenna-circuit loss, feeder loss and receiver internal noise. For a linear receiver free of spurious responses, the standard combines these in linear noise-factor units; at 290 K the familiar (P_n=F_a+10\log_{10}b-204) dBW follows. These are not the same quantity as receiver noise figure (F_r), link-budget (N_0), signal-to-noise ratio, ADC dBFS/Hz, or a spectrum-analyzer floor.

For a physical receiver, document at least: sensor/antenna terminals; antenna-factor or effective height/area and impedance; ground/earth, height, orientation/polarization, nearby structures and cable screening; feed and preselector insertion loss vs frequency; LNA/receiver NF or noise temperature; gain/AGC state; calibrated ADC scale and sample rate; effective noise bandwidth/window/detector; and the sample plane at which each PSD or APD was measured. Convert dBm/Hz to dBFS/Hz only using measured gain and ADC full-scale calibration, while documenting complex I/Q convention and whether PSD is per real component or total complex power. Integrate PSD using equivalent noise bandwidth, not nominal symbol or one-sided frequency span by assumption. Do not add dB quantities; combine independent noise powers in linear units. If coherent/correlated sources exist, power addition can be wrong as well.

P.372-17 explicitly excludes noise arriving over other conducting paths, inadequate screening/balance, and service signals from its external-noise categories. Thus “antenna noise,” conducted/electrostatic pickup, local oscillator/mixer noise, front-end spurs, and intentional/scheduled radio emitters should not be collapsed into one environmental noise curve. The true input-equivalent point is a mathematical equivalent lossless antenna; instrument readings at the LNA, ADC, IF or DSP plane require corrections and stated transfer functions.

At small VLF loop antennas, a calibrated magnetic field (B) is not automatically convertible to electric field (E) or available antenna power by free-space plane-wave relations. Ground-wave/near-field, Earth-ionosphere guided, and local reactive coupling can violate (E/H=377\,\Omega) or (E=cB). A loop response depends on effective area, turns, orientation and matching; self-resistance/coil loss and LNA voltage/current noise can compete with external magnetic noise. The VLF antenna chapter's cited measured deployments show use of crossed orthogonal loops and discuss low-frequency limits to field conversion. Treat (B\) spectrum and antenna-terminal voltage as separate calibrated observables unless the propagation regime validates conversion.

At 5.8 GHz, use a dish/beamsystem antenna temperature rather than assuming an all-sky hemispherical noise level. (T_A\) is the beam-weighted brightness integral; sky, atmosphere, ground spillover, feed/waveguide loss, antenna/LNA/mixer noise and Moon contribution should be kept separate. Atmospheric absorption and noise temperature co-vary. JPL's DSN handbook makes (T_{op}=T_{AMW}+T_{SKY}) explicit and defines its microwave antenna contribution to include spillover, LNA and waveguide terms; it tabulates site distributions only for its own stations at 1.7/2.3/8.4/25.5–27/32 GHz. It is primary operations literature, but it does not validate interpolation to an unknown amateur 5.8-GHz chain.

## Distinct frequency and environment cases

### Sub-9 kHz

P.372-17's Figure 1 covers 0.1 Hz to 10 kHz and presents minimum/maximum expected hourly medians for micropulsations and atmospheric noise. It says in that band seasonal, diurnal and geographic variation is comparatively small, while the larger 100–10,000 Hz variability is due to the Earth-ionosphere waveguide cutoff. Those are global/legacy standard summaries, not a calibrated 4–9 kHz local loop-noise distribution; and the current P.372-18 details could not be retrieved.

Measured VLF evidence shows why the in-band waveform statistics matter. Reuveni et al. (2010) recorded one minute out of every 15 minutes over 386 days at a remote Negev site, with two orthogonal 81 m² loops sampled at 100 kHz and calibrated frequency response. They produced 75-Hz bins at 4, 7.5, 10 and 26 kHz. Their (V_d=20\log_{10}(H_{rms}/H_{avg})) is an envelope-spikiness measure, not an absolute PSD or an APD. It is about 1.05 dB for circular complex Gaussian noise, often 2–3 dB for moderately impulsive cases and can exceed 10 dB for highly impulsive ones. In their 386-day Vd dataset, separate hourly/seasonal distributions fit single Gaussians with different means and standard deviations; the pooled long-period data needed a two-Gaussian mixture. Their 7.5 kHz mean varies by time/season (and their 4 kHz examples show strong hour dependence). The authors also note weak transmissions can be detectable between sferics but lost during sferic events. Therefore a long-window mean PSD or pooled (V_d) alone misses burst timing and usable gaps.

The global natural source is lightning sferics propagating in the Earth-ionosphere waveguide; impulses disperse and overlap. This creates colored, non-Gaussian, and temporally clustered interference. Man-made and local sources add distinct regimes: mains-frequency harmonics and power-electronic switching; electric-field coupling from nearby wiring; magnetic induction into loops; broadband electronics; and individual carriers/humps. In many modem allocations 50/60 Hz mains fundamental lies below the wanted band, but harmonics and switching spectra may enter it. P.372's antenna path cannot account for direct cable/structure coupling or poor common-mode rejection. Do not call the Earth-ionosphere field “white,” and do not equate noise level with impulsiveness.

For antenna/front-end characterization, use calibrated field or antenna-terminal spectra plus event statistics. A high-sensitivity narrow loop may be externally atmospheric-noise limited in one geometry and coil/LNA limited in another; rotating or swapping the orthogonal axes, checking common-mode, disconnecting/terminating the sensor, and comparing spatially separated sensors distinguish natural waveguide fields from local pickup. Preserve the receiver filter's actual impulse response because it changes observed sferic amplitude, width and event overlap. Reuveni's 75-Hz bin products are illustrative only; a deployed modem's waveform and equivalent bandwidth need its own measurements.

### Around 1 MHz: MF groundwave and skywave noise

P.372-17's man-made outdoor WGN category curves are summarized as median (F_{am}=c-d\log_{10}f_{MHz}), with city (c=76.8,d=27.7); residential (72.5,27.7); rural (67.2,27.7); quiet rural (53.6,28.6); and unshielded galaxy (52.0,23.0). In this edition, those curves are stated over 0.3–250 MHz, but category-specific caveats apply; they describe the WGN-like environmental component, not a generic narrowband RFI or total radio service occupancy. Atmospheric lightning noise is separately specified by local time, season, month/hemisphere and frequency charts, and its ionospheric propagation makes the local external noise time-varying.

The ITU-R SM.2449-1 (2024) impact report compares P.372 environmental estimates with Netherlands man-made-noise measurements at MF, placed at least 10 m from nearest building wall. At 1.65 MHz, its converted-to-field table reports city P.372 median −37.56 dBµA/m with 8.4 dB SD versus measured −26.23 dBµA/m with 6.4 dB SD; residential P.372 −41.86 ±5.8 versus measured −32.34 ±5.5 dBµA/m. Thus these measured medians are roughly 11.3 dB (city) and 9.5 dB (residential) higher than the standard values in that setup. They are not a universal correction: the report uses two project datasets and field conversions, and the measurements seek the radio-amateur user environment. It is strong counterevidence to treating legacy category medians as an up-to-date local site prediction.

At ~1 MHz, use groundwave propagation calculations for field strength only under their propagation domain; include a separate external-noise field based on actual hour/site/antenna. Groundwave branch notes P.368 should only be used where ionospheric reflection amplitude is negligible; night skywave may contaminate MF received noise and apparent range. P.372-17's below-foF2 qualification means one should not add the unshielded galactic curve as an actual 1-MHz sky source. Nearby broadcasters, amateur/industrial emissions, switched equipment and powerline communications may be spectrally selective or modulated and should be modeled as signals/interferers with occupancy, not silently smeared into an AWGN floor. P.372 excludes signals associated with radio services from the noise categories.

The correct operational question at 1 MHz is the noise/interference arriving at the real receiver after its antenna, grounding, preselection, feed and local RF surroundings; there is no support for a universal “MF noise number.” A time-frequency occupancy survey with calibrated power and an APD/line analysis is needed, with different day/night, busy/quiet, antenna orientation, and local equipment states. A receiver can show an apparent high noise floor due to local emissions, feeder common mode, internal cross-coupling, AGC or blocking even when an external ambient-noise model is lower.

### 30 MHz and HF skywave

P.372-17 Eq. (15) gives unshielded median galactic noise (F_{am}=52-23\log_{10}f_{MHz}) dB through about 100 MHz; it states that the Milky Way gives about ±2 dB variation on that curve, and a narrow beam toward the galactic pole is 3 dB below the all-sky average while directions toward the center can be stronger. The document says galactic noise is not observed below (foF2) and is lower than the unshielded value up to about three times (foF2). Man-made reflected noise also depends on (fxF2), which varies by time, season and solar cycle; above that frequency the ionosphere becomes more transparent and galactic noise can dominate. At 30 MHz the galactic curve is only a viable conditional boundary, not a fixed receive-floor setting.

Using P.372-17 predecessor equations (reproducible output below), at 30 MHz outdoor median man-made (F_a) is 35.9 dB city, 31.6 residential, 26.3 rural and 11.4 quiet-rural. The unshielded galactic curve is 18.0 dB. That calculation says an urban WGN-like environment may exceed galaxy strongly; rural also exceeds it in the curve; quiet rural sits below it before ionospheric reduction. It does **not** include atmospheric lightning/noise, a local interferer, site-specific antenna response, or v18 edition details. A quiet rural receiver can move from receiver-noise-limited to external-galaxy-limited simply with front-end NF and sky pointing, whereas urban broadband/narrowband interference and strong local transmitters can dominate both.

At HF the noise components may be comparable. P.372-17 §7 sums individual noise powers statistically, with half-normal distributions for component noise figures converted to log-normal noise factors; it uses prescribed decile deviations. The same standard warns total power alone usually is insufficient except for white Gaussian background: it calls for probabilistic waveform descriptions/APDs for non-Gaussian noise. Its category-time and category-location deciles are marginals, not a measured joint distribution of storm state, ionospheric state, RFI, receiver gain and signal outage. The receiver stress matrix must not create a severe joint event by independently combining every maximum unless that joint episode is physically observed or otherwise justified.

Directional antennas change which atmospheric and celestial sources contribute. P.372-17 says narrow-beam HF pointing can vary atmospheric-noise (F_a) by about ±5 dB, depending on direction, frequency and location; this is independent of skywave propagation variability. Man-made noise may travel through the ionosphere (per P.372 curves) and directional receiving antennas can reject it, while local feed/cable pickup may not follow the antenna main beam. Repeating antenna rotation with fixed cable layout and orientation, and measuring polarization and field arrival where possible, separates sky distribution from local pickup.

### 5.8 GHz EME

At microwave frequencies the noise budget is an antenna/receiver system-temperature problem rather than the MF/HF WGN-category formula. P.372-17 §4 says above ~2 GHz the Sun and a few bright sources deserve attention; the quiet-Sun value is at least (10^4) K at 10 GHz and increases during disturbance, while lunar brightness is nearly frequency-independent above 1 GHz and varies roughly 140 K at new Moon to 280 K at full Moon. The CMB is 2.7 K. At 5.8 GHz the Sun can overwhelm the normal floor if in or near the beam; when aligned for EME, the Moon also contributes a beam-diluted thermal foreground. A source larger than or comparable to beamwidth creates a substantial (T_A), not merely a faint modulation of an assumed receiver floor.

I ran a conditional main-beam sensitivity using a uniform 0.5-degree lunar disk and Gaussian circular dish beam with approximate HPBW (58\lambda/D). At 5.8 GHz this gives HPBW ~5.00, 3.00, 1.00 and 0.50 degrees for 0.6, 1, 3 and 6 m dishes. Approximate lunar coupling is 0.007, 0.019, 0.159 and 0.500 respectively; multiplying the P.372-17 140–280 K disk brightness yields lunar antenna-temperature contributions ~1.0–1.9, 2.7–5.3, 22–45 and 70–140 K. This is explicitly an idealized main-beam integral, not a measured antenna. Real taper, pattern, elevation, polarization, Moon phase/limb/thermal lag, sidelobes, feed spill, ground temperature and pointing error alter it. For small dishes, receiver/feed and ground spill can exceed Moon noise; for large antennas, Moon dominates over 2.7 K CMB and can be a useful pointing/calibration source. The EME branch independently reports an observed >2 dB lunar-noise change at 5.76 GHz and a 2024 operator log; it should be cited from that branch as real-world corroboration once its primary link and setup are documented.

NASA/JPL DSN Telecommunications Link Handbook 810-005 Module 105 Rev. E separately models atmospheric attenuation/noise temperature distributions, effective cosmic sky noise, and antenna-microwave noise (spillover, LNA, waveguide). Its own data apply to DSN sites and listed S/S/X/K/Ka bands; there is no direct 5.8-GHz or amateur chain calibration. Around 5.8 GHz, microwave gaseous attenuation and atmospheric noise depend on elevation, humidity and weather; high elevation reduces air mass but changing weather changes both. Record Moon/blank-sky/ground/Sun measurements by pointing, elevation and weather; do not transfer DSN dB/K figures blindly.

## Noise and interference are not interchangeable

ITU-R P.372-17 distinguishes (a) external atmospheric noise from lightning, (b) cosmic/galactic/solar/lunar sources and atmospheric gas/ground noise at microwave frequencies, (c) outdoor/indoor man-made WGN, and (d) measurement of the waveform's amplitude probability distribution. Its outdoor man-made section allows three coexisting mechanisms: WGN-like sum of many comparable distant sources; impulse noise; and single-carrier noise (SCN) consisting of a carrier or spectral hump from one/few sources. ITU-R SM.1753-2 likewise treats WGN, impulsive noise and SCN as distinct measurement components, and its APD example has Gaussian, impulse and line components visibly separated. A single mean square cannot determine interference protection or receiver decisions in these latter cases.

Common examples and their algorithm impacts:

* **Narrowband/coherent lines:** local clocks, broadcasts, switching harmonics, external tones or deliberate jammers. They can bias energy/quiet tests and produce leakage or false matched-filter peaks. Remove/project only if the nuisance hypothesis is explicit and both desired/alternative likelihoods account for it; notches can erase a real carrier or distort multicarrier data.
* **Impulsive/sferic noise:** lightning, spark, switch, motor/ignition or powerline communication activity. An impulse becomes the analog/filter chain's impulse response, with time/frequency spread; a one-sample IID random impulse is just one simplified stress. Higher rate can make pulses overlap and appear more Gaussian, so variance and impulsiveness need not move together. Preserve APD plus pulse width, burst rate and occupancy at the actual sample/filter bandwidth.
* **Cyclostationary / correlated noise:** periodic mains or switching waveform components, periodic transmission networks, and lightning sferic trains/propagation modes can create repeated spectral/time structure and correlations. Do not assume independent Gaussian errors across adjacent FFT bins, carriers, time windows or receive polarizations without measurement. Prefer measured covariance/spectral correlation or a clearly conditional model. Evidence on VLF atmospheric noise found some variation correlations at sites hundreds to about 1,500 km apart at night, but not 4,500 km and not a universal correlation length; Vd itself did not vary monotonically with lightning activity.
* **Receiver-created noise:** LNA/mixer/oscillator noise, quantization, clock jitter/phase noise, dither and gain changes. These need a device calibration or measured datasheet/chain. Do not add an independent “receiver NF” after already incorporating it in an ADC-plane empirical floor.
* **Signals and nonlinear artifacts:** blockers, compression, reciprocal mixing, harmonics and intermodulation are not additive external noise. They can desensitize the receiver or create in-band products and must be tested in the front-end path.

At VLF, NIST's classic bandwidth experiment shows that an isolated lightning impulse's response is set by the receiver filter: for bandwidth ratio (B), when normalized impulse-response shape is preserved, the response voltage scales by (B) while duration and exceedance probability scale inversely; observed high-voltage tails agreed for probabilities below 0.01 under its limited setup. This is a direct warning that an APD or impulsive stress cannot be detached from its receiver bandwidth. ITU-R SM.1753's impulse measurement method lists measurability limits (e.g. lowest pulse 13 dB above WGN, shortest duration one sample interval), so recorded “no impulse” below threshold is not proof no impulse exists.

## Front-end limits and discriminating checks

P.372's linear noise-factor equations assume a receiver free from spurious responses. They do not characterize nonlinear or time-varying front ends. The source/device-specific check suite should separate these failure mechanisms:

1. **Noise figure / source loss:** measure antenna-external noise on a currently unoccupied channel and compare to the same system terminated in 50 Ω with identical gain/filter settings. Correct in linear power for internal equipment noise when needed. SM.1753-2 says a WGN input at least 10 dB above equipment floor avoids an added correction for RMS measurement; use its equation/procedure or an uncertainty budget when not. At VLF the sensor input may not be 50 Ω or lossless; use its calibrated equivalent circuit/field factor instead. Include LNA, coil, matching network, feed loss and temperature.
2. **Filtering and aliasing:** record analog passband/stopband and full filter transfer function ahead of each sampling/decimation step. AN-282 from Analog Devices states anti-alias rejection must be related to the desired dynamic range and out-of-band input/noise must be attenuated before sampling; out-of-band tones/noise can fold into band. A downstream digital filter cannot remove a signal that already aliases onto a wanted sample frequency. Sweep blockers/noise across Nyquist zones using the real analog filter and sample-rate chain, measure in-band spur/noise. Current simulation's one-pole filter is a bounded model, not the ADC's alias rejection or receiver selectivity.
3. **Quantization/headroom:** use the actual analog gain and ADC full-scale, ENOB/noise spectrum, interleaving spurs, clipping code, dither, and analog peak-to-average distribution. The current synthetic quantizer supports ideal step/ENOB and clipping but does not model code-dependent spurs, jitter, aperture errors or high-frequency alias content. Run same noise/APD and blocker cases at several calibrated levels; log clipping/overload samples and post-overload gain recovery. A rare pulse can clip at fixed RMS power; Gaussian crest-factor tests do not bound sferic/SCN tail behavior.
4. **AGC:** log gain state against time alongside calibrated input/output. A large out-of-channel interferer can trigger AGC and attenuate a weak in-band signal; AGC memory/attack/release can span symbols/slots. Exercise continuous recordings through blocker-on/off and transient recovery, with same input and two gain-state settings. The current slow AGC and synthetic gain-step/overload recovery are useful notional effects; their constants are not calibrated device behavior.
5. **Blocking/compression:** test a weak desired waveform with a single in-band/out-of-band blocker swept in frequency and level; measure desired signal loss, noise rise, desense, clip/AGC state, and recovery. Record at the antenna, preselector, LNA and ADC reference planes where possible. The blocker level at the RF input is not the generator's front-panel setpoint; cable/filter/combine loss and mismatch must be measured.
6. **Intermodulation:** use a two-tone pair and modulated blocker combinations with actual spacings around wanted channels; measure second/third-order products at wanted frequencies and receive-bit/quiet metrics. P.372's AWGN sum is not valid for resulting deterministic in-band intermods. A stated IIP3/P1dB is only usable if valid for the exact band/gain state/temperature and should be checked against measurement.
7. **Phase noise / reciprocal mixing:** measure LO phase-noise spectrum at offsets matching expected blockers; add blocker at controlled power/frequency and measure in-band noise rise. Analog Devices' ADRV9001 UG-1828 documents that RX LO phase noise mixes a strong nearby blocker into the channel, degrading blocking; it is a device-specific demonstration, not a universal number. Synthetic Wiener/white-PM in the app represents oscillator impairment, but no blocker-dependent reciprocal-mixing path is documented in permitted code. For first-order estimates, (P_{mix}/Hz\approx P_{block}+L(\Delta f)) when SSB phase-noise (L) is specified in dBc/Hz and the blocker is not close enough for varying (L) over the bandwidth; integrate over wanted bandwidth and retain calibration/sideband convention. Use measured source noise floor to avoid attributing generator noise to the receiver.
8. **Carrier/polarization/site correlation:** rotate/point the antenna or compare orthogonal channels while holding feed layout, receiver gain and location fixed. Spectral lines that remain fixed when pointing changes suggest local/coupled source; sky/celestial noise that tracks antenna beam suggests external scene. For simultaneous channels, preserve their joint complex covariance and event coincidences. Marginal percentiles from separate runs do not prove simultaneous multi-carrier severity.

### Bounded sensitivity checks and their interpretation

`noise_sensitivity.py` evaluates only P.372-17 median external-noise curves and an ideal Gaussian beam approximation. The actual recorded output is in `noise_sensitivity.out`. It computes (kT_0=-173.975) dBm/Hz for 290 K, adds the P.372-17 (F_a) dB to the lossless-reference-antenna thermal PSD, then integrates a 3 kHz rectangular equivalent-noise bandwidth. Result at 1 MHz: city −97.18 dBm/Hz and −62.40 dBm/3 kHz; residential −101.48 and −66.70; rural −106.78 and −72.00; quiet rural −120.38 and −85.60; unshielded galaxy −121.98 and −87.20. At 30 MHz: city −138.09/ −103.32; residential −142.39/−107.62; rural −147.69/−112.92; quiet rural −162.62/−127.85; unshielded galaxy −155.95/−121.18 dBm per Hz/3 kHz. These values only show regime contrast; atmospheric noise and ionospheric visibility are omitted. In particular the unshielded galactic 1 MHz number is not physically observable where it is blocked below (foF2), and category curves are not full total-noise distributions.

Actual measured dish HPBW and sidelobes should replace the approximation. These calculations are not product performance, receiver sensitivity, or hardware qualification.

## Simpler alternatives, failure criteria and priorities

1. **Reuse** existing native AWGN/noise-seed and front-end simulation for fixed-input receiver ablations, preserving it as a clean reproducible baseline. Add tests of signal and noise at declared physical input levels only when the mapping is calibrated. If the goal is a pure decoder sensitivity curve, a Gaussian normalized case may be sufficient and easier than full RF environmental simulation.
2. **Adapt** a measured-trace/stochastic observation layer (not a second receiver): store calibrated PSD and amplitude/event summaries by frequency/site/time band; inject calibrated noise and interferers into the existing native source through a single channel interface. Favor real measured traces for severe-tail case studies and a small parameterized sferic/line family for controlled robustness. Preserve event timing and filter response. Do not sample a fixed P.372 percentile as if it were a universal independent Gaussian deviation.
3. **Keep simple deterministic interferers** for regression: single tone, impulse train/burst, broadband colored/WGN and gain step are useful unit scenarios.
