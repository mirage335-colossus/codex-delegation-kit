# Sub-9 kHz antenna and coupling evidence

## Decision handoff

For the modem’s default geometry, the carrier spacing is 75 Hz and the occupied baseband span is 225 Hz (`inner_rate_hz=36`, 10 Added Symbols; current geometry code is `src/triad_modem_v3.cpp:125–160`). A conventional compact antenna whose loaded response is a tuned resonance will usually not be flat or phase-linear across that span at 3–9 kHz. Under a simple one-pole response, meeting even 1 dB amplitude ripple requires loaded Q below about 6.8 at 3 kHz, 11.3 at 5 kHz, and 20.4 at 9 kHz. A 10° phase-ripple criterion is stricter: Q below about 2.35, 3.92, and 7.05, respectively. Many tuned loop, mechanical, and electrically small antenna systems exceed these bounds. In the absence of actual antenna/feed/electronics measurements, use a complex frequency-dependent transfer/calibration per physical antenna axis and include its drift, or explicitly constrain the deployment to one qualified narrowband mode with retuning. Do not infer that a decoded or simulated baseband signal saw a physical RF antenna.

Receiving should default to a calibrated crossed-loop magnetic-field pair for radiative VLF paths, with an optional third magnetic axis and/or electric-field/electrode sensor when the use case includes short-range induction, ground coupling, or uncertain local polarization. Put a low-noise, high-linearity front end at the sensor, and measure antenna + transformer + amplifier + cable + ADC as one complex transfer. Loop reception need not maximize power transfer when the environmental field noise dominates receiver noise. Preserve both raw axes and a defined vector/polarization combination; an RSS combination discards phase/polarization information.

Transmit choices are conditional on intended distance and installation:

- For true local inductive/through-earth service over tens to hundreds of metres, a current-driven loop or resonant mechanical/magnetoelectric radiator can be practical; specify this as a coupling link with measured H/B vs distance, depth/material, orientation, current, temperature, occupied band, and receiver sensitivity. It is not a free-space link budget.
- For radiative Earth–ionosphere-guide launch over regional/global ranges, a tuned, high-current, top-loaded vertical with extensive ground system (or existing large infrastructure) is the demonstrated family. Its radiation resistance competes with conductor, coil, insulator/corona and ground loss; accepted transmitter power is not radiated power. A compact loop’s extraordinary near field does not establish long-range guide launch.
- Horizontal line/wire radiators can support experiments under favorable ground and site conditions, but their launch efficiency, polarization, and narrow bandwidth are strongly site/load dependent. Existing lines and earth-electrode paths need measured common-mode/ground-current and radiative-field characterization; voltage at electrodes is not a universal received-field unit.
- Mechanical antennas are promising compact emitters/sensors, but published communication distances often use quasi-static 1/r² or 1/r³ magnetic fields and lab/near-field receivers. Keep them as a separately named, measured coupling option until a calibrated far-field or guide-wave demonstration covers the modem bandwidth and receiver.

The current simulation documentation warns that the modeled samples/noise are software inputs, not a calibrated DAC voltage, field strength, or antenna response; it explicitly separates software checks from physical-device qualification (`docs/channel-models.md`, “Baseline and channel stages” and “Interpretation limits”). Thus current results can be reused for modem/baseband receiver logic after matched transfer injection, but cannot be reported as a sub-9 kHz physical link or antenna qualification.

## Operating regimes and observables

At 3–9 kHz, the free-space wavelength is approximately 100–33 km. A 1 m loop or short whip therefore has `ka << 1`; a 100 m structure is still only a few thousandths of a wavelength. The operating question must distinguish at least three regimes:

1. **Reactive/local magnetic or electric coupling.** For a small current loop, magnetic moment is `m=N I A`. In the magnetoquasistatic dipole region, axial field scales approximately as `B ≈ μ0 m/(2πr³)` (orientation-dependent vector coefficients apply off axis); induction voltage at a receive loop is `Voc ≈ ω N_rx A_rx B_normal`. Strong local B and successful reception at short range are compatible with negligible radiated power. A conducting medium changes the field through diffusion/skin depth and boundary conditions. Treat soil, rock, seawater, depth, return current, spacing and alignment as part of the channel, not as a free-space path loss.
2. **Induction-to-radiation transition.** A dipole term includes near-field terms proportional to `1/r³` and `1/r²` and a radiation term proportional to `1/r`; the scale `r≈λ/(2π)` is where induction/radiation terms become comparable for an idealized source. This is roughly 5.3–15.9 km across 9–3 kHz. The Earth–ionosphere guide and lossy/stratified ground make that free-space boundary only a reference scale, not a universal far-field criterion. At closer distances, field ratio, phase and polarization may not obey plane-wave `E/H=η0`.
3. **Radiative/guide-wave arrival.** At sufficiently large range in the Earth–ionosphere waveguide, a field meter can be calibrated to the local wavefield and normalized against transmitter current and range, but propagation and ground conductivity affect the result. Use measured `E`, `H/B`, polarization, frequency and phase at a stated location and time. Do not convert loop voltage to `E=cB` unless the arrival is demonstrated to be a propagating free-space-like wave and the path/range conditions support that approximation.

Field quantities should use RMS (or clearly state peak) and SI units. For a receive loop with effective area `NA`, `Voc_rms ≈ 2π f μ0 N A H_normal` for a sinusoidal uniform field when the sensor is sufficiently small; include winding impedance, loading, preamp input impedance, transformer ratio and frequency response in the measured complex factor. For an electrically short E probe, use `Voc ≈ E·h_eff` only after calibrating effective height, capacitance, loading and orientation; nearby conductors and ground plane alter it. An earth-electrode pair measures a voltage gradient/conductive path plus capacitive pickup and contact impedance, not intrinsically a calibrated radiative E field.

A field result must identify its plane/reference: transmitter PA output; antenna tuning-network input; antenna current and voltage; measured local H/B or E at a surveyed point; receiver sensor terminals; preamp output; ADC input; or normalized software samples. Report accepted real input power and the separate estimated/observed radiated or guide-launched power. For a lumped resonant radiator, `η_rad = R_rad/(R_rad+R_loss)` is a useful circuit approximation only if `R_rad` and every loss term are referred to the same antenna-current plane and `R_rad` excludes ground/coil/conductor loss. A remote field/currents method can estimate an *effective* radiation resistance, but propagation and site errors enter it.

## Antenna and coupling families

### Top-loaded verticals and large VLF arrays

The conventional long-range VLF radiator is a grounded vertical wire/array with a large top-hat, loading coil and extensive earth/radial system. Top loading raises effective height/current near the top and reduces the short-wire penalty, but leaves large capacitive reactance, high circulating reactive energy, and small `R_rad`. Ground loss and coil/conductor/insulator losses can exceed radiation resistance. High transmitter current is useful only if current distribution and radiation resistance are known; a matching network can make input impedance look convenient while dissipating most accepted power.

The U.S. Navy NWC performance report provides an unusually explicit field-normalization example (1971 measurement report, pp. 1–4): its large array was measured at 22.3 and 15.5 kHz using calibrated loop substitution field meters and an independently transported thermal milliammeter; 11 sites at about 20–65 km were used, contaminated sites were avoided, loop nulls checked local anomalies, and induction field was included in analysis. At its closest site (10.85 km), induction contributed only 0.16 dB. Measurements were normalized to 1800 A and 100 km and followed an inverse-distance trend; resulting effective `R_rad` was 0.143 Ω at 15.5 kHz and 0.329 Ω at 22.3 kHz. These are not portable antenna constants; they show how large infrastructure can achieve useful but still low resistance and how the claimed quantity is tied to current, range, meter calibration, site screening and model assumptions.

For a generic transmitter, use measured tune-network input `V/I` and real input power, antenna current, temperature, coil loss/Q, ground-system loss, corona/insulator state and field measurements at multiple azimuth/ranges. The ground-current/radial network and soil conductivity are required environmental parameters. High voltage at the top load and coil can arc or corona; thermal limits can dominate before a semiconductor PA's nominal power. Retuning frequency shifts loading, current distribution, impedance and field; estimate the settling guard from actual tuner dynamics and resonator ring-down, not a software-only channel setting.

### Small loops and ferrite/core loops

Small loops primarily sense magnetic flux and reject much of the local electrostatic/electric-field interference when balanced and shielded correctly; they are directional with nulls. Orthogonal N–S/E–W loops capture horizontal magnetic components; retain raw channels and calibration. Fraser-Smith (2007) derives loop-specific antenna-noise conversion and shows the single vertical-loop direction blind spot; crossed loops combined by the square root of summed squares give approximate horizontal azimuth coverage, while this scalar combination removes polarization/phase. It is incorrect to apply a short-monopole electric antenna noise factor directly to a magnetic loop measurement.

Small-loop transmit radiation resistance is extraordinarily small. The ideal small-loop relation is approximately `R_rad = 31200 (N A/λ²)² Ω` for a uniform-current loop, where `A` is single-turn area. The accompanying desk calculation shows that a 10-turn, 1 m² loop at 5 kHz has ideal `R_rad≈2.4×10⁻¹³ Ω`; even a 10-turn, 1000 m² loop gives about `2.4×10⁻⁷ Ω` in free space. These values explain why compact conventional loops are normally receive sensors or short-range induction couplers, not efficient global VLF transmitters. The formula neglects nearby ground, magnetic core, loss, current redistribution and non-small geometry; a core changes magnetic moment/field and loss but does not erase the need for measured field/radiated power.

A receive loop's low radiation resistance does *not* mean its receiver must be thermal-noise-limited or require 50 Ω power matching. The induced voltage can be buffered with an appropriately low-noise high-input-impedance stage; transformer matching can improve the noise match for the real loop/preamp but adds winding loss, resonance and phase response. Fraser-Smith's field measurements, BAS practice, and the 2010 Negev VLF measurement system all put calibration at the full-loop + electronics response. The BAS Antarctic sensor uses two 56 m² single-turn loops, a low-noise preamp, 1.8 km separation from station equipment, and in-band calibration current through small orthogonal coils; at 5 kHz its typical sensitivity is `10⁻³¹ T²/Hz` (about 0.316 pT/√Hz) and is normally limited by global thunderstorm noise rather than receiver noise.

The 2025 nanocrystalline-core loop paper demonstrates a different compact high-current compromise at exactly 7.8 kHz: a 51 cm diameter toroidal-core loop, 1200 W claimed operation, and measured 47.32 dBµA/m at 120 m (about 232 µA/m or 292 pT in free-space `B=μ0H` units). Its measured field was 4–5 dB below its calculated/simulated field, attributed by the authors to nonlinear high-voltage core impedance; their test site was noisy. Communication to 1446 m at 0.16 pT is extrapolated from 1/r³ using a single 120 m maximum measurement distance and is not a measured far-field link or BER demonstration. Treat the result as a promising local-field power-handling option. Require current/voltage/temperature, harmonic distortion, field-vs-range exponent, range/soil condition, antenna loading and actual modem-band transfer before using it to predict coverage.

### Electric probes, grounded electrodes and earth-current systems

A short vertical conductor/capacitive probe responds to vertical E with an effective-height and capacitive source impedance; active E probes can be broadband when followed by a sufficiently high-impedance, quiet buffer. Ground-electrode pairs, long wires, power-line corridors and earth-current systems can collect larger potential differences or excite conductive/return paths. They may be useful in a local environment, through ground, or with a large existing structure, but the measurement includes soil conductivity, grounding impedance, electrode contact potential, lead/cable common mode, local field distortion and the return geometry. Electric and magnetic near-field components have different spatial falloff and phase. An apparent “antenna range” from voltage alone can be conductive/inductive coupling, pickup of mains/industrial common mode, or a true propagating wave; those hypotheses require separate measurements.

Rusch's 1959 Caltech thesis is a grounded sub-9 kHz counterexample to an assumption that only a tall vertical works: a resonantly loaded eight-mile commercial power line operated at 8.4 kHz and produced successful transcontinental detections. But the thesis concludes efficiency was only a few percent even on very favorable, high-resistivity granitic ground, unsuitable for high-power long-range service; frequency range was only about ±50 Hz due to high-Q line traps; audio-frequency interference and signal stability were practical problems. It documents a site-dependent horizontal radiator, not a small portable electrode antenna. Horizontal polarization and radiation changed with ground conductivity; the ground must be part of the antenna model.

For earth/electrode alternatives, log electrode spacing, depth, material, contact resistance, soil conductivity vs moisture, loop/wire geometry, current return, cable routing and common-mode current. Measure Hx/Hy/Hz and Ex/Ey/Ez (where feasible) with a calibrated probe at several distances/heights/azimuths; repeat with 180°/90° antenna rotations, a disconnected/sham lead, reversed electrode polarity, and changed electrode spacing. A true field response rotates/polarizes according to geometry; mains/cable artifacts often follow wiring and site. Do not use volts-per-metre calibration from one local electrode setup as the sensitivity of another.

### Mechanical, magnetoelectric and direct antenna modulation

Mechanical emitters create an oscillating electric/magnetic dipole by moving charge or a permanent magnet, or by piezoelectric/magnetostrictive strain. They can avoid a bulky high-reactance conductor/loading network and show strong local fields or useful coupling through conductive media. Their resonant mechanical Q, modulation bandwidth, actuator energy, nonlinearity, frequency drift, mechanical/package size, drive voltage, switching transients and magnet/cantilever losses are distinct constraints. A narrow mechanical resonance cannot be declared broadband by quoting high Q; direct antenna modulation can switch the resonant state, but requires active tuning/switching and must preserve phase, occupied band and settled response.

Kemp et al. (2019, *Nature Communications*) is foundational primary evidence for a lithium-niobate strain-based VLF transmitter: 9.4 cm × 1.6 cm rod; measured ring-down `Q≈300,000`; simulation for a 14.1 mA·m dipole predicts 125 kV internal crystal voltage and 120 mW average dissipation. Direct antenna modulation shifts resonant frequency with FSK state to bypass passive bandwidth constraints. The paper describes a conceptual demonstration and modeled efficient transmission; its near-field magnetic B decays as approximately 1/r², consistent with an electric dipole, and not as a long-range plane-wave `1/r`. It cites an antenna-size example needing a 10.5 H / 125 kV matching inductor for a 10 cm, 35.5 kHz short dipole—an illustration of infeasibility for conventional lumped matching at that scale, not a sub-9kHz modem-ready component.

Recent magnetoelectric (ME) results include resonant 2FSK receiver sensitivities and 1–60 m lab/near-field ranges, and 2023 MME transmitters whose field was measured from 0.1–5 m and fit to `1/r³`; the authors estimate extremely small total radiative efficiency. These are evidence of promising sensor/coupler devices, not yet evidence of replacing a large guide-wave transmitter. The 2025 sub-9kHz nanocrystalline loop is similarly measured near-field. A recent Nature Communications wearable rotating resonator table gives some examples with `~100 fT at 100 m` and low rates; however, values must be compared only after matching frequency, input power, B-axis/orientation, bandwidth, detector sensitivity and near-/far-field regime. Keep these methods as optional physical channel modes, not as an assumed common antenna response.

## Matching, bandwidth, calibration and retuning

For a passive, single-resonance antenna around `f0`, an idealized normalized voltage response is

`H(Δf) = 1 / (1 + j 2 Q_L Δf/f0)`.

This is a useful discriminator, not a substitute for measured antenna impedance or a multi-resonance/active network model. With total occupied width `B=225 Hz`, the edge is `Δf=112.5 Hz`. For maximum allowed edge amplitude droop `D dB`, require `Q_L ≤ (f0/(2Δf)) sqrt(10^(D/10)-1)`. For maximum edge phase error `φ`, require `Q_L ≤ (f0/(2Δf)) tan φ`. The reproducible desk check `checks.py` evaluates this at 3, 5, 8, and 9 kHz. At 5 kHz, the 1 dB amplitude bound is Q≤11.3; the 10° phase bound is Q≤3.92. At Q=25, the edge is −3.55 dB / −48.4°; at Q=100 it is −13.3 dB / −77.5°. Therefore a single scalar field calibration is only suitable when the deployed signal is sufficiently narrow relative to the characterized loaded response and phase/gain ripple tolerances are documented. A three-carrier receiver should ingest the complex transfer over all carrier frequencies and sidebands, including absolute delay and inter-axis response; equalization can only repair this where SNR, receiver headroom, and the measured inverse are adequate.

A residual resonant-frequency drift is an additional `Δf` at every carrier. Temperature, mechanical stress, ferrite/core permeability, ground moisture, component aging, antenna motion and bias current can move resonance or change losses. Log these inputs and use an injected known field/tone to track response. For loaded resonator Q, field/energy amplitude ring-down time is approximately `τ=Q_L/(πf0)`; 1% settling is `4.605τ`. At 5 kHz, Q=100 gives τ≈6.37 ms and 1%-settling≈29.3 ms; Q=300 gives ≈88 ms. Actual multi-pole tuner, switch, high-voltage amplifier and mechanical resonance settling may be longer and must be measured. Add this to any frequency-hop guard or reject the hop until the measured transient clears. If analog tuning changes the transfer, the receiver needs a per-hop transfer state and acquisition/guard accounting.

Chu's 1948 lower bound and later small-antenna theory show the severe size–radiation-Q tradeoff for a passive radiator enclosed by a small sphere. At `ka<<1`, radiation Q rises approximately as `1/(ka)^3` (the precise bound depends on mode, directivity, and assumptions). Chu's idealized radiation-Q bound is not itself a practical bandwidth/efficiency promise: material and circuit losses, ground, conductor and matching-network limits worsen the outcome. Bode–Fano limits constrain how well a passive lossless network can match a specified reactive load across bandwidth; adding loss can widen apparent match while reducing radiation/received signal, and non-Foster active circuits trade bandwidth for active noise, stability, power, and calibration. Yan et al. (2017) empirically increased measured −10 dB S11 fractional bandwidth from 4.7% passive to 18% using tunable negative inductance on a 1×1 m receive loop (15–30 kHz), but measured average gain +1.1 dB and no SNR advantage across that band; the active matching noise exceeded its gain benefit with the low-noise receiver. A good S11 or input voltage is not a sensitivity or radiated-efficiency metric.

### Calibration and field normalization

For a receive calibration, sweep amplitude and phase over the complete antenna axes and receiver band with a known field, not only a generator into an unloaded preamp. Magnetic field calibration can use a characterized Helmholtz/solenoidal coil; state coil geometry/current, uniform-field region, reference sensor, alignment, uncertainty, and included/excluded electronics. Electric sensors need a known field electrode/plate geometry or traceable effective height; also measure input capacitance and response. Preserve complex frequency response, cross-axis matrix, cross-talk, temperature, gain, ADC quantization/linearity/clipping, and receiver noise PSD at the same reference plane. Recheck after site deployment because nearby metal, ground, mast, cables, and preamp placement alter response.

For a transmit field test, measure actual antenna feed current/voltage/real power and field amplitude/phase at multiple surveyed distances and azimuths. Record height, soil/site conductivity, terrain, ground system, weather/moisture, transmitter frequency/modulation, tuner state and calibration uncertainty. Look for a stable distance law and far-/guide-wave polarization before converting to an effective radiated power or antenna efficiency. Plot `H` and `E` independently; if the measured ratio/phase departs from the radiative relation or scales closer to `r^-2`/`r^-3`, classify as near-field coupling. A single field value at one distance is not a link budget.

## Receiver interference, dynamic range and operational feasibility

The antenna/front end sees sferics, narrowband VLF transmitters, power-line harmonics, switching supplies and local wiring, plus optional high-latitude chorus/hiss. Natural atmospheric noise is impulsive rather than Gaussian: in Reuveni et al. (2010), 386 days of 75 Hz-bin data at 4, 7.5, 10 and 26 kHz show envelope voltage deviation `Vd = 20 log10(Hrms/Havg)` commonly around 2–3 dB for moderate impulsiveness and >10 dB for highly impulsive conditions. At 7.5 kHz daily means ranged 4.8–8.2 dB; long-term Vd was better represented by a two-Gaussian mixture while hour/season groupings were single Gaussian with changing parameters. A stationary AWGN receiver floor will not model an impulse's overload, recovery or erased intervals. Preserve measured PSD plus APD/percentiles, crest factor/Vd, impulse rate/amplitude-duration distribution, narrowband occupancies, and overload/recovery—not just total RMS noise.

A Stanford front-end description notes femtotesla-scale signal detection, strong nearby VLF navigation/beacon signals, front-end distortion/overdrive and the need for high linearity/dynamic range. The operational receiver often uses a transformer plus custom low-noise amplifier and automatic tuning to compensate component drift/temperature. A 2022 flight receiver paper reports 90 dB dynamic range over 50 kHz and 1 fT/√Hz sensitivity as a specific design achievement, not a universal requirement; a 100 dB ground VLF setup is also reported in literature. These cases justify measuring blocking/compression, intermodulation, overload time, ADC full-scale use and recovery under co-channel transmitters and strong sferics. Locate the antenna/preamp away from noisy equipment; the BAS system used 1.8 km separation and the Finland survey receivers were >9 km from power lines and settlements. Such distances are site recommendations, not mandatory universal spacing.

Crossed loops give robust horizontal magnetic coverage but an RSS combination erases phase. For polarization-sensitive multi-carrier reception, keep calibrated complex N–S and E–W channels; optionally include vertical H and an E probe. Silber et al. (2015) found vertical H sometimes comparable to or greater than horizontal components at one terrestrial site, contrary to simple waveguide expectations. They list local ground inhomogeneity/reflecting boundary and ionospheric anisotropy as candidate mechanisms; local machinery also appeared in the spectrum. The study is unusual, not proof vertical H is generally large. The correct decision is to make missing-axis sensitivity explicit and test it in deployment, not hard-code zero vertical field.

## Conditional engineering plan and discriminating checks

1. **Reuse now:** continue to reuse canonical C++ waveform, continuous receiver, and synthetic impairments for baseband design. Preserve their normalized units. Expose a documented antenna/sensor transfer interface (complex response by axis/frequency, optional time-varying calibration, reference plane) and inject it into the existing sampled channel only when measured/calibrated. Keep physical front-end, channel/environment, and software receiver reports separate.
2. **Ask for missing deployment inputs:** center frequency and legal/hardware band; carrier configuration; allowed PA real power/current/voltage; antenna size/material and intended installation; ground/soil/medium and return geometry; range/depth/orientation; antenna tuning/hop strategy; transmitter duty cycle; receiver sensitivity/noise figure/blocking and ADC range; calibration resources; environmental temperature/moisture. Without these, selection of a physical antenna is conditional.
3. **Small matched bench discrimination:** use 3-axis magnetic + calibrated E sensors and the intended antenna/front-end. Sweep complex response across center ±112.5 Hz and the full occupied band, with temperature/load variation. Test tuner resonance and ring-down using the actual hop waveform. At a fixed carrier, compare calibrated vector fields/decoded modem outcomes through loop, E probe, electrode pair (when allowed) and null/sham wiring; keep input SNR and reference plane matched.
4. **Classify coupling:** measure H and E amplitude/phase at multiple near/far ranges, azimuths, and heights; fit alternative `r^-3`, `r^-2`, `r^-1` trends with uncertainties and inspect whether E/H approaches radiative relation. For earth/current paths measure conductivity, electrode/return geometry, and depth. A result that changes materially under antenna rotation, cable replacement, or placement farther from power systems falsifies a free-space field interpretation.
5. **Transmitter power/thermal:** simultaneously record feed current, voltage, real input power, coil/core/capacitor temperature, harmonics, and field. Compute real losses and duty-cycle thermal margin. A large field that tracks reactive current but disappears with range/soil or lacks radiative scaling supports local induction only. Verify each carrier's state during switching and measure overload/settling; reject any transmit strategy whose tuner cannot settle within public guards.
6. **Decision rule:** accept scalar calibrated antenna gain only if full-band measured gain ripple/phase and drift fit the allowed channel error budget; otherwise apply measured complex transfer or declare only a single-frequency/retuned operating mode. Accept a radiated-link claim only after multi-range current-normalized fields establish radiative/guide propagation and a separate receiver BER/availability result. Treat all other ranges as measured coupling envelopes with a stated medium, geometry and threshold.

The reusable checks are in `checks.py`; stdout was saved as `checks.txt`. The calculation is analytical, not a product/receiver run or a physical qualification. `checks.py` evaluates the 225 Hz resonance response, ring-down and its settling estimate, ideal small-loop radiation resistance, the λ/(2π) distance scale, loop-induced receive voltage per pT, and the 2025 measured-field conversion/extrapolation. Assumptions and limitations are explicit in the equations above and in the script. Current antenna/site/center frequency and the receiver’s allowable channel ripple, phase error, hop guard, and BER threshold remain unresolved dependencies.
