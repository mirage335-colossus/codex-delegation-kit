# Sub-9 kHz propagation: regimes, observables, and a model boundary

## Decision handoff

**Recommendation: adapt the channel around a frequency-dependent complex path transfer, with an optional physical propagation teacher; do not treat sub-9 kHz propagation as a parameterized two-ray fading channel.** The current official ITU-R P.684-8 methods explicitly cover frequencies below about 150 kHz: waveguide modes are the recommended VLF/low-LF method through about 60 kHz; within VLF they use ray/wave-hop plus ground-wave theory below 1,000 km and a mode sum above 1,000 km. That 1,000 km split is a numerical-method recommendation, not a physical discontinuity. The 3–9 kHz band straddles the Earth–ionosphere waveguide's first several modal cutoffs. Below roughly 1.6–2.2 kHz the lowest quasi-TEM mode dominates the propagating waveguide solution; above that, modes enter and interfere. Near-source reactive fields and short-path evanescent modes add another regime. At longer distances the least attenuated modes dominate, and the received field can vary strongly with ground conductivity, ionospheric state, magnetic-field direction and mode phase. A single path delay / Doppler spread does not encode those dependencies.

For candidate link calculations, use a layered model with (i) a source/near-field and local coupling boundary owned jointly with antenna/noise work, (ii) a lossy-ground surface component, and (iii) an Earth–ionosphere guided transfer. The ITU-R wave-hop calculation vector-adds ground diffraction and one or more sky hops at shorter VLF ranges; its mode calculation sums complex guide modes at longer range. Both can produce field phase/amplitude, subject to their assumptions. A full-wave or mode solution should produce complex H(f) across the actual carrier band. Estimate or simulate receive level, phase and group-delay slope from that transfer; add a stochastic residual only after conditioning on path, frequency, local time, season, geomagnetic direction and disturbance state. For a very small modem bandwidth, a calibrated per-carrier complex gain plus a measured local phase slope may be the smallest useful receiver-facing interface. Keep outage as a distinct state/measurement. Do not infer absolute field strength from the propagation literature without transmitter current/antenna, distance, receiver effective height/area, losses, and reference plane.

**What this chapter changes:** it rejects direct extrapolation of the old 8–30 kHz Wait–Spies tables down through all sub-9 kHz conditions; establishes low-band physical evidence from Cummer et al.'s broadband ELF/VLF study, DEMETER cutoff observations, and an [8.971 kHz transatlantic carrier detection](https://www.arrl.org/news/radio-amateur-s-sub-9-khz-vlf-signal-detected-across-the-atlantic); and identifies a calibration confound between ionosphere and mixed land/sea conductivity. That transatlantic record demonstrates actual VLF waveguide detectability at ~6,194 km, at an estimated 150 μW ERP, over both daytime and nighttime paths, with 12.5 dB S/N in 46 μHz at night and visibility in a 23 μHz daylight bandwidth. It is a narrow single-path demonstration, not an environmental or calibrated modem-channel distribution. The most discriminating near-term check is matched amplitude *and phase* versus frequency and path distance, including one near-cutoff band and one above-cutoff band, under logged day/night and ground-path conditions. A two-ray model should be retained only as an abstract receiver stress model or if actual measured transfer residuals show it is adequate for the target paths.

## Three spatial/frequency regimes are different physical questions

Let (k=2\pi/\lambda). For a small-source free-space field, (kr\ll1) is the reactive near-field scale, (kr\sim1) is a transition, and (kr\gg1) is the radiation-field limit. The corresponding scale (r\sim\lambda/(2\pi)) is a *dimensional marker*, not a hard boundary for a ground-coupled antenna or a stratified Earth–ionosphere guide. A source-receiver spacing of 500 km is about 0.83 wavelengths at 500 Hz, 2.8 at 1.7 kHz, 5 at 3 kHz, and 13–15 at 8–9 kHz. It can therefore be radiative in the ordinary (kr) sense while still in the short-path, many-mode regime of the guide. “Far field” must name the reference: antenna reactive field, short-range guide modes, or asymptotic long-range guide field.

At low frequencies a second distinction matters. At 500 Hz, free-space wavelength is about 600 km; at 1 kHz it is about 300 km. Induction, electrostatic, earth-current, surface-diffracted, and waveguide fields can all contribute, depending on source, range, observation component and frequency. A direct local electric or magnetic coupling path, a conductive return path through soil/water, and a guided far-range path have different transfer functions and calibrations. Do not combine them into one random “fading” multiplier. For a land or sea half-space the good-conductor skin-depth approximation is \(\delta=\sqrt{2/(2\pi f\mu_0\sigma)}\). It gives, for example, 7.96 m at 1 kHz for \(\sigma=4\) S/m seawater, 159 m for 0.01 S/m, and 503 m for 0.001 S/m; at 8 kHz these are 2.81, 56.3 and 177.9 m. This is an illustrative homogeneous-half-space sensitivity, not a site estimate, and very low-conductivity ice/stratified geology can violate the simple boundary assumptions.

The source-to-receiver propagation also transitions with range. The ground-wave arrival and first ionospheric hop are separable at short ranges for impulsive lightning recordings, but overlap after a few hundred kilometers; larger-range waveform peaks then reflect interference and higher hops rather than a clean “direct plus one echo” geometry. A global lightning-network study estimates ground-wave isolation coverage at about 600–800 km in its particular instrumentation and processing context; this is not a universal modem boundary. ITU-R P.684-8 recommends hop theory for VLF paths under 1,000 km because the mode series converges slowly there, and the mode method beyond 1,000 km because the modal sum converges rapidly; it also says hop theory can work in some long-range cases down to 10 kHz. Treat these as computation choices whose accuracy depends on path, not a guaranteed modal transition. At still longer range, only a few low-loss modes often remain, but complex amplitudes and phases of those modes continue to depend on path conditions.

The script `regime_scales.py` makes these scales reproducible. Its first-order parallel-plate formula gives (f_n\approx nc/(2h)). For effective heights 70, 85 and 90 km, the idealized first cutoff is 2.14, 1.76 and 1.67 kHz respectively. At 3 kHz the ratio (f/f_1\) is about 1.4–1.8; at 8 kHz it is 3.7–4.8. Hence a 3 kHz carrier lies near the first higher-mode transition while an 8 kHz signal can involve several higher modes. Real mode cutoffs and attenuation differ because the ground and ionosphere are lossy, dispersive and magnetized; this relation is a scale check only. DEMETER's observed first-cutoff band of 1.6–1.8 kHz independently supports the order of magnitude, not the exact parallel-plate model.

### Nearby regime boundaries and evidence

* **Reactive/source-coupled zone:** diagnose by measuring the field with multiple calibrated E/H components versus distance and orientation, along with source current and coupling. A spatial (1/r^3\), (1/r^2\), or radiative (1/r\) law should not be imposed through a ground/earth-ionosphere interface without deriving the geometry. This short-distance physical regime is an engineering inference from Maxwell's near-field scaling; the low-frequency waveguide sources below mainly treat dipole radiation or lightning, not the project's unspecified direct-coupling hardware.
* **Short guide / transition zone:** full-wave fields can include evanescent or highly attenuated modes that a truncated far-range mode sum omits. Cummer et al. explicitly state that such modes can matter at short distances and mode theory becomes less efficient there (their 2000 paper, §II-B, pp. 1423–24). Their impulsive 1000 km calculations show a multimode spectrum with peaks and null-like interference. At <500 km and below about 500 Hz, lightning post-discharge ionospheric relaxation fields can also matter; those are not part of standard harmonic mode theory.
* **Long guided path:** the solution is a sum of modes with complex propagation constants; a small number of low-attenuation modes often suffices at long range. The amplitude scales roughly with cylindrical/spherical guide spreading times exponential attenuation (with curvature correction), and phase accumulates per mode. Real range-dependent transfer should come from a mode/full-wave calculation or measurement, not free-space Friis.

No evidence here establishes a universal near/transition/far range cutoff for an actual modem antenna. A suitable cutoff is the distance where the predicted field decomposition changes *and* measured E/H component ratios or complex transfer stabilize under a validated solver. Antenna branch can establish source size, matching and coupling transitions; propagation branch must supply surface and guide changes.

## Ground boundary and alternative propagation mechanisms

The lower waveguide boundary is conductive but not perfect. For a uniform ground model, complex surface impedance depends on conductivity, permittivity, magnetic permeability and frequency; the field penetrates to a conductivity-dependent depth. In heterogeneous paths the surface impedance changes at land/sea/ice boundaries, so modal excitation, attenuation and phase change continuously, and the transition can cause conversion among modes. Wait's older theory represents the lower surface imperfectly conducting; modern global studies confirm it can matter. The current science does not support treating all “groundwave” as a single conductive mode independent of the upper guide: at short range it can coexist and interfere with ionospheric hops; at long range the guide is the more useful organization.

Teysseyre et al. (2025), *Radio Science*, is a targeted model study of a finite-conductivity segmented path, not a new global field-measurement survey. It uses Wait–Spies two-parameter ionospheres; the authors explicitly say (H'\) and \(\beta\) are best viewed as a waveguide parametrization, because inferring a richer physical electron-density profile from a few transmitter amplitude/phase observations is ill-posed. In its 24 kHz scenarios, path inhomogeneity changed fitted ionosphere parameters; for 10% electron-density retrieval precision at 70/90 km, the study says conductivity detail at roughly 10 km may be required in some tested cases, and at 70 km a low-conductivity island around 50 km or larger (or a salty-lake segment about 20 km or longer) can matter. Those are scenario-specific inverse-problem sensitivity results; do not promote them as universal map-resolution requirements for a communication link. The authors also find frequency trends that complicate a simple “higher f always more conductivity-sensitive” story: lower frequency increases ground penetration and can require more spatial detail, while at a fixed frequency poor conductivity raises excitation and attenuation. Their stated 12–40 kHz sweep does not directly qualify <9 kHz.

For an alternate empirical route, Said et al. (2023), *JGR Space Physics*, use a year of global lightning-location-system paired amplitudes (unknown source power cancels in simultaneous same-event sensor ratios) to estimate broadband attenuation factors. They report the saltwater-to-ice ground-conductivity differential spanning about 12 dB/Mm; attenuation has geomagnetic east–west asymmetry and day/night dependence. Eastward sferics attenuate about 1.5 dB/Mm less at night than by day in their fitted cases; daytime westward paths near the geomagnetic equator attenuate about 2.8 dB/Mm more than eastward paths. But at low magnetic dip angle, westward nighttime paths can have so weak an ionospheric reflection that their fit cannot isolate ionospheric attenuation: the ground wave persists and the assumed separable attenuation basis breaks down. This is important contrary evidence against a single path-independent attenuation coefficient. The product is a fitted broadband impulsive-lightning attenuation model, not a narrowband calibrated link budget and not a sub-9-only data set.

**Decision:** condition attenuation and phase on path segment conductivity or measured transfer. Start with a compact coarse path class (sea/ordinary land/low-conductivity ice plus fraction and sequence), not a huge digital terrain model. Add segmentation where sensor residuals or a recognized boundary materially changes the link. Falsify the extra segmentation if a matched complex-transfer comparison shows no decision-relevant gain over a path-average surface. Keep the ionosphere and surface parameters separately identifiable in experiments; otherwise an H′/β fit can absorb ground error.

## Earth–ionosphere guide, modal dispersion and polarization

The current [ITU-R Recommendation P.684-8](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.684-8-202208-I!!PDF-E.pdf) (08/2022) is the international field-prediction reference: its scope is below about 150 kHz, with waveguide mode theory intended through about 60 kHz and wave-hop methods at higher LF. It asks for transmitter and receiver geometry, path, frequency, ionosphere parameters, season/day/time, and terminal ground electrical properties; finite conductivity changes terminal vertical radiation patterns. The sub-1000 km VLF hop method sums ground wave and one/more sky paths vectorially; the >1000 km mode method computes a complex modal sum. The mode calculation allows a smooth homogeneous Earth with adjustable conductivity/permittivity, Earth curvature, vertical ionosphere profiles, anisotropy and arbitrary electric-dipole orientation; it returns attenuation, phase velocity, mode excitation, field amplitude and phase. For gradual horizontal changes P.684 recommends WKB modal sums; for day/night terminators or large land/sea conductivity changes it calls for mode-conversion methods. Its mode-conversion slab assumes invariance transverse to the path and neglects horizontal reflection; its accuracy is limited by this approximation. Annex 2 advises a default day Wait profile beta/H′=0.3/74 km until additional seasonal/solar/frequency results are available, but includes detailed nighttime parameters varying with frequency/latitude and directs users to VLF conductivity maps. It notes the wave-hop method lacks worldwide verification; its 60 kHz daytime comparison has no measurement to compare. This is a sound model family and parameterization reference, not measured sub-9 channel data.

Spies and Wait's [NBS Technical Note 300](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-C13-4647e5540c69c7ae371f0cfc8598744e/pdf/GOVPUB-C13-4647e5540c69c7ae371f0cfc8598744e.pdf) (1964) is a foundational curved-Earth mode calculation, but its published numerical results cover 8–30 kHz, and its examples idealize the ionosphere as sharply bounded/homogeneous and magnetized. They invoke the quasi-longitudinal approximation and note it is good along the magnetic meridian or in polar regions for all directions; arbitrary magnetic dip treatment is called relatively crude. This is valuable near 8–9 kHz, but the report alone does not justify all sub-8 kHz conditions.

An independent [Longwave Mode Propagator study](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10319646) by Gasdia & Marshall (2021) implements a complex-root mode solver and supports arbitrary dipole orientation, electric-field components, terrain/ionosphere segments and mode conversion. Its reported validation frequency range begins at about 5 kHz, making it a candidate offline reference at 5–9 kHz rather than a validated model for the entire 0–9 kHz band. In one modeled four-slab day/night transition, LMP, LWPC and EMP2D agree well; however, LMP misses a vertical-E field null beyond 500 km for a low-sharpness profile H′=70 km, beta=0.15 km−1. That specific failure indicates nulls can be solver/model-sensitive; verify complex transfer around them with an independent full-wave reference rather than making every null an unconditional channel feature. Their FASTMC/FULLMC conversion treatments omit backward reflection from a later discontinuity. P.684 likewise warns its conversion slab ignores horizontal reflections. This matters for repeated boundaries or strong contrasts; do not use the simplified segment result as an exact multiple-reflection solution.

Cummer et al. (2000), *IEEE Transactions on Antennas and Propagation*, compare mode theory and FDTD for ELF/VLF. The ionosphere is a cold plasma with height-varying electron density, collision frequency and Earth's magnetic field; day/night profiles differ. Its 2-D transient source is a vertical current at ground and its receiver observes E/H fields at distance and altitude. The paper's mode/FDTD spectra and Fig. 4 comparison with measured multi-lightning ELF/VLF spectra reach across the 0–20 kHz range and thus supply useful <9 kHz support, but they are broadband lightning fields, not a calibrated narrowband transmitter link. Their FDTD grid is 1 km; they did not verify convergence and attribute above-10 kHz disagreement partly to grid resolution. Below 10 kHz they report very good agreement between their FDTD and mode solution. The paper's simplified analytical models have narrower conditions: e.g., the vertical-B approximation is good at ELF but badly changes the VLF spectrum above ~1.5 kHz, while omission of the magnetic field is very poor for nighttime conditions. Some conductor approximation is acceptable above ~1.5 kHz in their nighttime comparison but is less accurate around daytime VLF near cutoffs. Do not import one simplification outside its demonstrated regime.

In mode theory, each mode is a complex eigen-solution determined by reflections from both boundaries. Its complex propagation constant represents both attenuation and phase velocity; the modal field at the receiver is their complex sum. Thus:

1. **Group delay and phase delay are not interchangeable.** At carrier (f_0\), observed phase φ(f_0) gives a phase-equivalent delay modulo one cycle. Waveform-envelope group delay is (\tau_g=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\,d\phi/df\), after phase unwrapping and reference-plane calibration across adjacent frequencies. A physical propagation delay is not generally one fixed tap delay across multiple modes or a wide band.
2. **Intermodal cancellation can masquerade as fading.** Near a mode-interference null, a small change of ionosphere, ground path, season or range can change amplitude sharply and cause rapid phase rotation. Close to a modal cutoff the group velocity is reduced and a delayed tail is dispersed. Cummer et al. show the frequency-response/time-response pair at 1000 km and describe the near-cutoff tail; the apparent “echo” is a structured modal response and should not be assumed to be an independently moving specular ray. A separate analytic stress calculation in `regime_scales.py` makes the group-delay hazard explicit: a direct path plus an opposite-phase echo with only 7 ms delay and 0.99 relative amplitude has −40 dB transfer at 8 kHz, yet the local phase derivative reports −693 ms group delay. This does not claim nature produces this exact channel; it shows that near a cancellation notch, a phase-slope estimator can yield an apparent delay far beyond either path support. Reject unqualified delay fitting at low SNR or deep nulls; propagate confidence and the complex transfer.
3. **Long-path simplification is conditional.** At sufficiently long range high-attenuation modes disappear, making few-mode estimates efficient, but remaining modes still interfere. The 2023 empirical attenuation study confirms that distance, direction and boundary conditions change even broadband envelope peaks, and notes overlap of ground and first-hop waveforms beyond a few hundred km.
4. **Polarization is vector-valued.** Vertical electric dipole excitation primarily launches TM (transverse magnetic) fields, but oblique magnetized ionospheric reflection converts polarization and rotates phase/amplitude among components. To measure polarization, a calibrated vector receiver needs orthogonal magnetic (and, where needed, electric) components with known mutual phase and orientation; a single scalar antenna does not measure polarization changes. ELF studies report ellipticity for vector components under day/night nonuniformity. Until the intended modem antenna has a known vector response, polarization is an uncertainty/interface field rather than an invented scalar fading law.

The standard continuous-wave VLF choice is a curved-Earth mode solver such as LWPC/ModeFinder given transmitter frequency, antenna/current or radiated power, surface profile, D-region electron profile or (H',\beta\), and geomagnetic field. It returns path phase and amplitude versus range. The accessible 2025 dawn/dusk work reports that the two US Navy modal codes usually give similar results under most conditions; both are used to fit measured phase and amplitude along paths. For localized, sharp disturbances or near-source short paths, full-wave FDTD is a useful reference but has computational and discretization costs. A mode solver is practical as an offline teacher for per-path synthetic fixtures; a calibrated table / reduced-order complex response can serve real-time receiver hypotheses.

## Diurnal, seasonal, geographic and disturbed conditions

### Ordinary day/night and terminators

The lower D region is the upper lossy waveguide boundary. Quiet daytime free-electron density is driven substantially by solar UV (including Lyman-α); nighttime electron removal/recombination raises effective reflection height. VLF beacons are often stable away from terminators, which makes their phase and amplitude useful ionospheric diagnostics. This stability is conditional: the path samples an extended strip, not a point receiver. A single average (H',\beta\) can hide changing terminator position and latitudinal/longitudinal variations along the great-circle path.

Thomson et al. (2025) analyze measured phase/amplitude through dawn and dusk and fit modal codes. At mid-latitudes their fitted (H'\) goes from ~85 km at night down rapidly to ~73 km near dawn (SZA ~−90°), back to ~78 km near SZA ~−75°, then to local noon values. Dusk is more monotonic from ~70–75 km toward ~80–85 km at night. Fitted sharpness β ranges ~0.25–0.6 km⁻¹, with transition minima and latitude/SZA dependence. At lower latitudes a dawn minimum was absent, attributed to less GCR contribution; GCR becomes more important at higher geomagnetic latitudes. This finding rejects a discontinuous binary day/night switch and also cautions against treating “dawn” as a single smooth interpolation independent of latitude.

The reported (H',\beta) are path/time fit parameters; for fixed SZA they are averages along the propagation path. In the same paper the station set includes 45.9 kHz and other higher VLF links, so quantitative values are evidence about the D region and model structure, not a calibrated <9 kHz field-strength law. The study's phase records needed phase-drift / phase-jump adjustments in some cases (transmitter/MSK effects), which is a reminder that transmitter reference and modulation phase must be corrected before attributing phase changes to propagation.

### Seasons, path, geomagnetic field and high latitude

Toledo-Redondo et al. (2012) analyze more than four years of DEMETER electric-field data and map cutoff-derived effective height. The first waveguide cutoff is about 1.6–1.8 kHz; at this mode cutoff losses are maximal and a spectral energy minimum appears. The effective height derived globally changes by 5–10 km seasonally in the southern warm-season South Pacific and twice-yearly near the geomagnetic equator in Atlantic/Indian regions; authors attribute variability mainly to solar radiation and thunderstorm activity, with multiple ocean/land mechanism explanations kept open. Satellite observations are local to the satellite sampling point, while ground paths average along the lightning-source-to-receiver route. This is low-band field evidence for systematic environment change, but it is not an amplitude/phase transfer link measurement.

Earth's magnetic field makes the ionosphere anisotropic; propagation eastward vs westward can have different reflection coefficient/attenuation and therefore different received amplitude. The 2023 lightning-network study measures/fits this asymmetry and its regime-specific breakdown. At high latitudes, galactic cosmic rays and auroral/energetic particle precipitation can be significant ionizers; solar proton events and relativistic electron precipitation alter received beacon amplitude and phase. South Pole and Arctic VLF studies compare these variations with particle observations, demonstrating that an out-of-family disturbance is not an arbitrary deep fade but a location/time-dependent lower-ionosphere perturbation. For the modem model, do not hard-code a single high-latitude loss: condition on geomagnetic latitude/dip and disturbance state, and retain a measured outage probability only if a cited route dataset supports it.

### Solar flare, geomagnetic storm, precipitation and lightning

Solar X-rays increase ionization on sunlit paths, lower the effective D-region reflecting height and shift phase (often advancing it); received amplitude can either rise or fall depending on modal interference. A flare response can be fast, strongly path-dependent and measured against GOES X-ray flux. Avoid converting all flares to one attenuation scalar: the result depends on path illumination, distance, time relative to terminator and baseline modal nulls. For intense geomagnetic storms, a 2025 Mediterranean study of three paths reports stable weaker day signals vs stronger fluctuating night signals, stronger variability for longer paths, and path-orientation effects; disturbance anomalies were stronger at night and delayed relative to a geomagnetic index in that event. This is a particular regional/event result, not a universal storm envelope.

Lightning has two roles: impulsive sferics are a broadband interferer and a source of transient waveguide excitation; lightning-induced electron precipitation can also perturb nighttime D-region conductivity after magnetospheric wave-particle coupling. In the broadband signal, direct ground and ionosphere-returned components can overlap with opposite sign and create waveform-dependent peak amplitude changes. This can bias an estimator that assumes a single tone or one delay. Keep lightning impulse interference and slowly evolving propagation-state disturbances as separate model terms.

## Observable conventions, uncertainty and use in a modem

* **Amplitude:** beacon-monitor literature often reports field strength (e.g., dB relative to 1 μV/m), spectral voltage, or a normalized receiver amplitude; lightning networks use field peaks/statistical ratios. These cannot be transplanted to the modem until antenna effective height or area, impedance/matching, cable/preamp/front-end response, polarization, reference plane and transmitter current or EIRP are known. Preserve a dimensioned field observable and an explicitly normalized complex channel; do not combine them.
* **Phase:** report reference source/timebase, carrier frequency, phase unwrap/ambiguity, transmitter modulation and oscillator corrections. In a 2003 NAA array study, amplitude and phase were averaged in a 100 Hz carrier-centered window; 1-s values were usually repeatable to ±3% amplitude and ±2° phase, but local interference and lightning atmospherics sometimes exceeded that range. These are measurement repeatability under that beacon/array configuration, not a propagation distribution. Phase referenced between sites also includes receiver-reference geometry.
* **Delay:** report arrival time from a calibrated reference waveform separately from group delay obtained from unwrapped phase slope. At one carrier, phase difference alone gives delay only modulo (1/f_0\); for example a full cycle is 125 μs at 8 kHz and 333 μs at 3 kHz. Fit adjacent coherent tones (or sweep) with shared frequency/time reference to estimate (d\phi/df\), and compare against a solver-derived complex response. If the transfer contains a deep null, the group-delay estimate is ill-conditioned; mark it uncertain rather than clipping or inventing a physical echo.
* **Frequency response:** for narrow tones, estimate complex (H(f_i)=Y(f_i)/X(f_i)) per carrier using synchronized TX/RX pilots and remove known instrument/antenna response. Fit phase slope only over a band with adequate SNR and unwrap confidence. Use a complex amplitude ratio across all carriers to distinguish mode interference from global gain drift. Adjacent-channel comparisons must retain absolute frequency and path identity.
* **Noise/interference:** VLF natural lightning noise and man-made interference vary in spectrum and impulsiveness; they are not thermal AWGN. This branch establishes propagation contributions, not noise distributions. Receiver/noise work should separately characterize local noise floor and impulse blanking; the propagation model must not silently absorb noise excursions into path fading.
* **Spatial reference:** use geodesic path distance, source/receiver coordinates and azimuth, geomagnetic dip and direction, path sunlit fraction/SZA history, season/date, and land/sea/ice segments. “Day” and “night” labels without path illuminance are inadequate near terminators or mixed paths.

## Compact physical stochastic-channel proposal

For offline channel appraisal, use parameterized state \(z(t)\) and a deterministic physical response \(H(f;z,\rho)\), where path descriptor \(\rho\) includes great-circle geometry, ground segments and magnetic direction. For VLF 3–9 kHz the response teacher is a mode solver with enough modes to converge amplitude and phase, validated against full-wave calculations where the path is short or local irregularities are strong. State \(z\) may start with (a) path-mean (H',\beta\) driven by local solar zenith/season/latitude, (b) measured/fitted quiet residuals, and (c) named event perturbations such as flare or precipitation. Use the full complex response to create per-carrier amplitudes/phases and, for waveform responses, inverse-transform across the occupied band. Store a deterministic baseline; draw stochastic residuals conditionally, not from a universal Rayleigh/Rician law.

Receiver-facing boundary can remain light: provide `complex_gain_per_carrier`, optional `group_delay_slope`, a validity/confidence flag, and optional `outage/erasure` state. Stream ownership must preserve phase across chunks, just as the current native streaming channel does. Keep oscillator drift and front-end transfer as separate factors so a propagation-state change cannot be fit away as RX frequency offset. A fallback two-path synthetic channel remains useful to isolate equalization/acquisition robustness, but label it `abstract_multipath_stress`; don't map its delay/Doppler to a particular VLF route without data.

Before adding a full model to the product, compare it against a much cheaper transfer library indexed by carrier set, range, path class, path sunlit fraction, latitude/dip and state. The table must retain complex phase and source/reference-plane metadata; interpolation across a mode null needs special care. An online model solve is not warranted for a continuously operating receiver unless it demonstrably improves decisions within the complete-receiver capacity budget.

## Discriminating checks and falsifiers

1. **Low-band solver coverage:** build two independently derived reference curves around the 1.6–2.2 kHz cutoff and in 3, 5, 8–9 kHz, with one day-like and one night-like profile, actual ground conductivity and magnetic geometry. Compare complex amplitude/phase from converged mode code and a bounded FDTD reference for short range. Reject a model/configuration if only magnitude matches or a guessed single ray replaces a measured modal notch/phase turn.
2. **Path-segment sensitivity:** compare homogeneous-sea, homogeneous-land, and mixed land/sea paths with otherwise matched ionosphere. Require the model to reproduce phase as well as attenuation; test whether a proposed coarse segment class is enough at the candidate route. Teysseyre's 24 kHz retrieval thresholds motivate a sensitivity test but cannot be treated as a sub-9 kHz answer.
3. **Measured complex transfer:** with coherent pilots at neighboring frequencies and a common timebase, record simultaneous calibrated E/H signals along a short/medium path and a longer path, across at least stable day, stable night and terminator passage. Fit complex transfer and group delay; repeat over several days/seasons. A physical model earns adoption if it predicts held-out carrier amplitude and phase better than a two-ray abstraction and a static scalar baseline under same antennas, waveform, reference plane and noise.
4. **Disturbance:** log GOES/solar X-ray, geomagnetic indices, particle/auroral context and path SZA with all observations. Test local flare, geomagnetic storm and high-latitude precipitation separately; don't combine their marginal maxima as a joint “worst case” without event observations. Estimate outage only from matched time-series / a defensible operational definition.
5. **Mode-versus-delay decision:** fit unwrapped measured φ(f) slopes across small frequency spans and compare with group-delay estimates from the mode transfer. If measured phase change aligns with broad gain/oscillator drift but not modal predictions, propagation model needs calibration; if steep slopes coincide with modal minima and measured amplitude/phase across a spatial array, preserve modal physical state. If only a few carriers are available, test ambiguity and uncertainty; phase alone at a single carrier cannot prove propagation delay.
6. **Polarization:** where antenna hardware permits, obtain calibrated orthogonal field channels with interchannel phase calibration. If no vector sensor exists, quantify the antenna's orientation response and carry polarization uncertainty in effective gain; do not claim a polarization-aware receiver benefit.

These are proposed validation checks, not checks run on the product. The executed desk calculation is `python3 research/vlf-propagation/regime_scales.py`, reported below. No project source was changed or built.

## Reproducible calculation and result

Conclusion from this desk check: the project's “sub-9 kHz” envelope is not one homogeneous propagation regime. At the low edge, ordinary free-space near/transition geometry and guide cutoff/resonance scales coincide with possible direct induction/coupling; by 8–9 kHz the source radiation scale is shorter but the guide still carries multiple modes. Soil/sea conductivity also spans orders of magnitude in idealized depth. This justifies a model boundary that accepts measured/site parameters and branches by regime. It does not prove a link budget or implementation throughput.

## Remaining evidence boundary

The 2014 W4DEX–Nicholson 8.971 kHz report is a public operator-reported carrier detection on one 6,194 km path, not a calibrated time-series of the full complex transfer and not a peer-reviewed link study. It does not give seasonal variation, outage statistics, near-cutoff response, adjacent-tone phase slope, or comparable site/antenna response in multiple directions. The receiver combined corrected E- and H-field outputs into a directional response to reject a strong southwesterly background, so measured level is receiver/antenna/noise dependent.

The synthesis lead also supplied a first-person [January 2015 RSGB archive report](https://klubnl.pl/rsgb_lf_group-archives/html/rsgb_lf_group/2015-01/msg00101.html) describing a 8.822 kHz, ~6,194 km coherent EbNaut message link with ~150 μW ERP, 8 s symbols, 25 characters, and receiver E/H combination. The lead’s summary reports a measured ~0.12 fT and post-sferic-blanking S/N of −13.2 dB in 0.125 Hz (estimated −56 dB in 2.5 kHz); the thread is a contemporaneous first-party operator post, but I did not inspect it within this branch’s web cap. Treat these values as promising direct-message evidence pending source-level review, with the same limits on path generalization and calibration. It demonstrates that sufficiently coherent, heavily coded/slow sub-9 communication can succeed on at least one long route, but says little about a modem’s throughput or robust availability.

The strongest other low-band evidence remains (a) measured ELF/VLF broadband lightning spectrum and modeled comparison, (b) satellite-observed low-band cutoff/seasonal height maps, and (c) general 3–30 kHz global attenuation observations whose operational band may be weighted higher than the target. These support the governing mechanism and need for complex transfer; they do not yield a general channel-gain, outage-tail, vector coupling or quiet-day distribution. Request actual candidate frequencies, antenna/sensor geometry, path coordinates, source current/effective radiated moment, occupied bandwidth and intended time/event envelope before selecting stochastic distributions. A physical response can still be tested conditionally with those as explicit inputs.
