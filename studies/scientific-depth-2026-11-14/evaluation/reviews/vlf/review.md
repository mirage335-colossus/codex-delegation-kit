## Independently checked evidence and calculations

* **Rusch (1959), primary full thesis:** [Caltech thesis record and complete text](https://thesis.caltech.edu/655/), “Long-Range Propagation Experiments,” pp. 117–120, and “Summary,” pp. 121–123. The thesis reports 8.4-kHz detection at Cambridge (2,600 miles) and Rocky Point, but says only bare signal presence was detectable there: field strength and stability could not be determined. The Cambridge receiver used 2–3 Hz bandwidth; the author estimated 100 W radiated, calls out the favorable unfractured Sierra granite, says even favorable-ground horizontal lines were limited to a few percent efficiency, and reports only about ±50 Hz frequency variation from high-Q traps. The fresh chapter’s treatment of this as a site-specific horizontal-line experiment, not proof of a small electrode or portable loop’s far-field range, matches the original and retains its boundary.
* **Ai et al. (2025), primary full publisher PDF:** [Nanomaterials article](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-4991/15/14/1062/pdf?version=1752109474), especially §§2.3–3, Fig. 11/12, pp. 11–13. It reports a 7.8-kHz, 10-turn, 51-cm loop and a 47.32 dBµA/m field measurement at 120 m; the test section says the tested range was limited to 120 m. The paper’s 1,446-m figure is calculated under an assumed (r^{-3}) decay and 0.16-pT threshold, not measured communication or BER range. The fresh chapter makes this key distinction. Independent conversion from the reported field gives (H=10^{47.32/20},µA/m=232.27,µA/m), hence (B=µ₀H=291.88) pT in air. (120(291.88/0.16)^{1/3}=1,466.26) m, about 20 m above the paper’s 1,446 m. The participant’s own check script also outputs 1,466.3 m but the chapter does not explicitly reconcile these two values. The discrepancy is small and affects only the conditional extrapolation; it should be resolved or left explicitly unresolved, not presented as exact agreement.
* **Yan et al. (2017), primary full text:** [Wiley article](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1155/2017/6198686), §§4.1–4.2 and conclusion. For a 1×1 m loop over 15–30 kHz, it reports average signal gain increase about 1.1 dB and no SNR improvement (average −2.6 dB) with its 4-dB-noise-figure measurement receiver. This is useful contrary evidence against equating active matching bandwidth/gain with improved receive SNR; it is above 9 kHz and its authors specifically distinguish receiver-noise conditions, so it is not a direct sub-9-kHz prediction. The fresh chapter preserves that applicability limit.
* **Calculated response and scale checks:** the independent script recomputes geometry from the cited current code, the one-pole response (H=1/(1+j2Q_LΔf/f₀)), the loop radiation resistance and induced voltage formulas. It reproduces the chapter’s Q limits at a 112.5-Hz band edge (for example, 1-dB Q≤11.308 at 5 kHz; 10° Q≤3.918), its Q=25 edge loss/phase (−3.5519 dB/−48.366°), and the reported ideal loop scale (R_r=2.414×10^{-13},Omega) for 10 turns × 1 m² at 5 kHz. The (lambda/(2π)) scales also reproduce. These are analytic controls with stated idealizations, not measured antenna performance. See [independent calculator](antenna_calcs.py) and [output](antenna_calcs.txt).
