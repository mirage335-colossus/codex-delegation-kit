# Groundwave near 1 MHz and 30 MHz

## Decision handoff

Use **ITU-R P.368-10 / its LFMF-SmoothEarth reference implementation** as the reproducible, low-cost field-strength baseline for a near-ground vertical-polarization path when the path is sufficiently smooth, transmitter and receiver are at/near ground, the ground constants are known well enough, and ionospheric reflections are negligible. This current recommendation is in force, was approved in August 2022, and covers 10 kHz through 30 MHz. Its upper endpoint is inclusive, but that does not make a 30 MHz path a long-range link or turn the method into a terrain/line-of-sight (LOS) model. P.368-10 explicitly says not to use it when ionospheric-reflection amplitude is not negligible or when the receive antenna is well above ground. The homogeneous model assumes smooth spherical Earth, a near-surface short vertical monopole reference source, and returns the vertical far-field component. See the [current recommendation and conditions](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.368-10-202208-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf), pp. 2–4.

At **1 MHz**, the groundwave is a plausible stable daytime or short-path component, but soil/sea properties strongly set its attenuation, and nighttime or distant ionospheric skywave can arrive on the same channel later and interfere. P.368 is conditional on those skywave reflections being negligible; it is not a day/night model. For broadcast planning, current P.1147-4 separately predicts statistical night-time skywave field at 150–1,700 kHz over 50–12,000 km; it uses annual medians of half-hour medians and a ground-level loop receiver convention. That makes it a planning reference for whether a 1 MHz skywave component may matter, not a real-time complex echo model. [P.1147-4](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.1147-4-200708-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf), §§1–3 and Annex 1. At **30 MHz**, P.368 provides the surface-wave baseline only over its own 10–30 MHz scope and near-surface conditions. The ordinary operating situation is often short-range LOS/reflection or terrain-diffraction, with groundwave competing as a distinct component; sporadic-E skywave can also occur. A path-horizon calculation is a useful geometry screen, never a reception cutoff.

Do not put a scalar P.368 loss on top of an already propagated free-space/two-ray channel. Choose one coherent interpretation of the physical transfer. A scalar P.368 curve gives field magnitude, not enough phase or delay information to coherently sum with a second ray. For the 1 MHz complex channel, either use/extend the official LFMF implementation to return complex ground attenuation, or explicitly declare that an amplitude-only field-strength envelope is being modeled and do not claim realistic phase, group delay, or multipath. A 2024 MF R-Mode paper demonstrates a complex attenuation/phase extension of the C++ LFMF reference code and shows that mixed paths can produce field phase recovery at land–sea boundaries; it also warns that conductivity-map error and unmodeled seasonal changes limit delay accuracy. That is adjacent evidence (300 kHz navigation), not quantitative validation at 1 MHz. [Hehenkamp et al. (2024), method and limitations](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/24/1/282).

Use path-referenced soil electrical properties, not a single global conductivity knob. P.527-6 expresses a lossy ground using complex permittivity (equivalently conductivity and relative permittivity) and documents strong dependence on moisture, temperature, freeze state, frequency and geological structure. It retains the conventional low-frequency σ/ε tables and curves used by P.368. For mixed land/sea or distinct soil sections, use the P.368 Annex 2 reciprocal Millington calculation (each section has its own σ and ε, and the forward/reverse dB predictions are averaged). The model captures the demonstrated “recovery” of field after a bad-conductivity section onto sea or good soil. It is still a smooth-segment approximation; do not claim boundary-scale phase or terrain accuracy from it.

At 30 MHz and on rough or built paths, check terrain explicitly. P.526-16 (2025) is the current diffraction reference. Its spherical-Earth method uses ground electrical properties, and its terrain methods require a path profile and refracted Earth geometry. At exactly 30 MHz P.1812-8 (2025) also starts in scope (point-to-area terrestrial prediction, 30 MHz–6 GHz); when detailed path profiles are missing, its own scope guide directs the user to P.1546 instead. These methods are useful coverage/attenuation comparators, not substitutes for a coherent groundwave phase response or site-specific short-link measurement.

Primary measurements show why a 30 MHz “LOS only” shortcut is unsafe. NTIA TR-84-151 measured daylight 28 MHz groundwave on low mobile receive equipment over paths up to 45 km. Terrain ranged from smooth to mountainous, forested and suburban. The study observed 20 dB typical spread in path median loss between different paths, 6 dB upper-to-lower decile spread for smooth paths and 13 dB for mountainous paths. A smooth-Earth versus path-profile WAGNER difference reached 15 dB at 28 MHz; in one path/model comparison the WAGNER residual to measured median reached 25 dB. Its results are site and apparatus specific (and at 28 MHz, not exactly 30), but decisively show that frequency alone does not define a link range or LOS state. [Adams et al., NTIA TR-84-151 (1984), measurements and comparison](https://www.its.ntia.gov/publications/details?pub=2158), especially §§2, 3, 8–9.

Recent contrary evidence should temper trust in generic groundwave curves in populated environments. Fockens et al. (2022) report measurements at 16 Dutch residential locations over 1.8–28 MHz, with greater losses than expected from the ITU groundwave model and a different roll-off. The accessible University repository gives only the abstract; its full-text PDF request returned 403, so no numerical residual or setup details are asserted here. This is a credible contrary finding, not enough evidence to replace P.368 with its regression. [Repository record and abstract](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/propagation-measurements-and-analysis-on-mf-and-hf-bands-in-urban/).

## Mechanisms and operating regimes

### Lossy-ground surface wave

For a vertical radiator near a lossy surface, fields are coupled to the interface. Energy penetrates and dissipates in the ground, and the surface-wave component follows Earth curvature beyond a simple straight ray’s horizon. Conductivity alone is insufficient over much of this frequency range: the medium wave number depends on both σ and ε. P.527 gives the constitutive relation

\[
k=\sqrt{-j\omega\mu(\sigma+j\omega\epsilon)},\qquad \mu\simeq\mu_0.
\]

The relative importance of conduction versus displacement current is indicated by σ/(ωε). The included calculation gives this ratio for representative P.368/P.527-style ground pairs: for “medium dry” illustrative values (σ=0.001 S/m, εᵣ=15), it is about 1.2 at 1 MHz but 0.040 at 30 MHz; for wet ground (0.01 S/m, 30), about 6.0 at 1 MHz but 0.20 at 30 MHz. For sea water (5 S/m, 70) it remains about 1,280 and 43, respectively. These are constitutive ratios, **not path-loss predictions**, but they show why blindly reusing a 1 MHz conductivity-only value at 30 MHz can be materially wrong. P.527 notes loam may be about 10⁻² S/m when moist and as low as 10⁻⁴ S/m when dry; moisture is a dominant determinant. At low frequency, conductivity changes about 3% per °C; freezing can produce a large decrease in both ε and σ. Seasonal surface moisture and geology matter.

In broad qualitative terms, the P.368 curves are nearly at the same free-space reference near 1 km and separate with distance; at a fixed path, higher σ/ε and lower frequency tend to sustain greater field. P.368-10’s Figure 1 gives multi-frequency sea water at σ=1 S/m, εᵣ=80; Figure 2 at σ=5 S/m, εᵣ=70. Curves for different ground constants at 30 and 60 kHz appear in Figures 4–5; separate Annex 1 software computes the full supported range. The diagrams are the model output; no synthetic terrain or modem test is implied here.

### Groundwave, space wave and skywave are different signals

1. **Groundwave/surface wave** is the lossy boundary-coupled component. Its path loss and phase depend on σ, ε, frequency, path length, polarization and curvature. The field is not simply a free-space 1/r ray.
2. **Space wave** comprises local direct and ground-reflected components in/near LOS plus their terrain diffraction. At low heights the reflected component can almost cancel the direct component at some frequencies/ranges and reinforce it at others; small spatial movement can make rapid ripple. This is coherent propagation, so combine complex fields before converting to power. Do not add a two-ray loss after a P.368 groundwave loss unless the source/receiver and included components were defined so that no physical component is counted twice.
3. **Skywave** is an ionospheric return with separate delay, time and frequency dependence. At 1 MHz, daytime D-layer absorption can suppress it while nighttime reflection often creates a strong delayed copy; then it can beat with groundwave, causing fading/phase changes and co-channel interference. At 30 MHz, ionospheric return is conditional (e.g., sporadic-E episodes), not a permanent floor. Any scenario with significant skywave violates the P.368-only prediction condition. A single, random Rayleigh fade cannot preserve the distinct arrival delay, coherence or mode-on/off behavior.

The exact transition among these mechanisms depends on antenna height, terrain profile and refractivity, ground impedance, path length and observation bandwidth. A path may be surface-wave dominated beyond nominal LOS; below the horizon a terrain-diffracted spacewave may still be observed. Near transmitter or receiver in the antenna near field, P.368’s radiation-field output needs its near-field correction or should not be used. At 30 MHz, P.526’s practical first-Fresnel-radius expression gives roughly 159 m at midpoint for a 10 km path and 337 m for a 45 km path; this is why ground/terrain is not a negligible perturbation for antennas a few metres high. P.526 says an obstacle in the first Fresnel zone can create diffraction even where nominal LOS exists. Our radio-horizon check yields about 11.7 km for 2 m/2 m antennas using k=4/3; 30 MHz waves do not abruptly stop at that number.

### Terrain and mixed boundaries

For a homogeneous, smooth Earth, spherical-earth numerical solutions (Sommerfeld/Norton residue-series family; current P.368 integral software) incorporate curvature and finite conductivity. Flat-earth/Norton approximations are cheaper and useful when range is small relative to Earth curvature and the modeled path is uniform; they do not represent horizon curvature or piecewise soil without extra boundary terms. A ray/two-ray calculation is inexpensive and useful for clear local spacewave paths when antenna heights and reflection coefficients are known; it fails in grazing, surface-wave-dominated, rough or blocked geometry and can predict deep nulls very sensitively to centimetres of path/range. A smooth-earth spherical method is more grounded for 1 MHz and 30 MHz than a free-space ray, but it cannot reproduce path-specific ridges, buildings or clutter by itself.

For **mixed paths**, Annex 2 of P.368 uses a reciprocal Millington method. For three sections it calculates field strengths on homogeneous curves forward and backward across boundary distances, then averages the two final dB field predictions. That is the logarithm of a geometric mean in linear field, and is not an arithmetic mean of received powers. The method matters because propagation arriving at a boundary has a vertical field distribution/wave tilt shaped by the preceding section: following a higher-conductivity section can “fill in” field after poor land, while leaving good land for poor soil can show an extra drop. The historical 1949 Millington/Kirke BBC measurements and later Aono–Muramatsu mixed-path sea tests document this effect. P.368-10 calls this Annex method the most accurate available and reciprocal; that is a standards claim about this calculation family, not proof that it equals full-wave boundary physics for every route.

Observed disagreements are actionable. Aono and Muramatsu’s January 1955 Tokyo Bay 1.85 MHz and 2.5/4 MHz field surveys report fill-in at sea entry; their paper notes Millington explained it better than older one-direction procedures, but could overpredict in some measured sections, while Furutsu fit those cases better. In another 1.85 MHz route, the sea-to-land drop was not well explained by those methods. A 2024 R-Mode phase map shows phase recovery across a coastline and phase varying about one-eighth wavelength around a constant-range arc; this is adjacent but direct evidence that boundary composition can affect phase, not only attenuation. For layered wet coast or a narrow saltwater/ice/snow transition, use segment-wise parameters as a first pass and keep the model uncertainty broad until a measured cross-boundary path supports a more elaborate integral-equation model.

At 30 MHz, 10 m is one wavelength; features that are small relative to 300 m at 1 MHz may be electrically large at 30 MHz. P.526’s terrain diffraction model or P.1812-8’s current point-to-area procedure are reasonable alternatives when a terrain database and path geometry exist. P.1812 is statistical in time/location and has terminal-height and service assumptions; it does not return a deterministic complex impulse response. P.1546 is an area-coverage empirical alternative if detailed profiles are unavailable. Compare their prediction conventions before combining; both represent total coverage/field, not an independent loss to tack onto P.368.

## Calibration, reference plane and field/power conventions

P.368’s reference is not “1 kW EIRP from an isotropic antenna.” The curves assume a short vertical monopole on a perfectly conducting plane radiating 1 kW, with field 300 mV/m at 1 km (300 V cymomotive force), and report vertical radiation-field strength. Its corresponding basic transmission loss is

\[
L_b=A_i=142+20\log_{10}(f_{MHz})-E_{dB\mu V/m}.
\]

The reproduced desk check converts 300 mV/m to 109.54 dBµV/m and obtains at 1 km 32.46 dB at 1 MHz and 62.00 dB at 30 MHz. These match free-space loss to 0.01 dB, which verifies the reference normalization and unit arithmetic only. This value is a reference field, not an environment forecast.

To compare with receiver data, report both field and antenna-plane power. If a calibrated incident field E is known and receiver antenna effective height is h_eff (complex and polarization-dependent), the open-circuit voltage is E·h_eff; loaded voltage/power then depends on antenna impedance, matching network and receiver impedance. Alternatively, power available from a plane wave can be calculated from power density and effective aperture only with actual gain, polarization mismatch and environmental coupling declared. Convert to ADC dBFS only after recording analog gain, filtering, impedance, front-end compression and ADC full scale. Do not equate a field-strength dBµV/m with dBm, or a normalized complex-IQ sample amplitude with either quantity without a calibration map. After this calibration, a power loss L dB acts on voltage-like complex samples with amplitude scale 10^(−L/20), while power ratio is 10^(−L/10). With fixed receiver noise variance, this makes SNR decrease by L dB. Applying the power ratio directly as an IQ amplitude would double the attenuation in dB; the included 20 dB example gives IQ amplitude ×0.1 and power ×0.01.

For groundwave measurement, calibrate source feed power and antenna radiation pattern/effective radiated power; log vertical antenna height, radials, ground screen, polarization/tilt, distance, position and receiver antenna response. Measure path ground constants at appropriate frequency/soil, not just a distant map. Measure the groundwave phasor relative to a coherent reference if delay/frequency behavior matters. Use frequency/time sweeps with a monitor transmitter or calibration loop to distinguish environmental propagation from TX/RX oscillator drift. Measure in a receiver bandwidth narrow enough to separate co-channel interferers where possible; separately record broadband PSD and impulsive/time-varying interference. A field median averaged over space or minutes must not be silently turned into a coherent single-path value.

P.368 itself warns of 3–4 dB (about 3.5 dB typical) local level differences for nearby receiving points and seasonal groundwave field changes of 5–15 dB in winter versus summer monthly median for 500–1000 kHz depending on climate. Those are not universal probability distributions; they show that point location and weather/season matter. The 2024 phase study states that static P.832 conductivity map inaccuracies are its largest AGDF error source and cannot quantify the predictable/unpredictable error ratio from maps alone. Treat calibration and ground-state uncertainty as model inputs, not noise margin chosen to “make the curve fit.”

## Measurement and model evidence

### Foundational and standards evidence

* **P.368-10 (ITU-R, approved 2022; in force)**: official current frequency/range, homogeneous spherical-earth assumptions, P.368 reference radiator, vertical E output, mixed-path Annex 2, error and seasonal notes. It specifies integral software as the prediction method because field-strength computation is complex. Its source package is separately linked from the recommendation page. Use this exact revision for new comparison; do not use legacy P.368-9/GRWAVE4’s broader 50 MHz application as current authority.
* **P.527-6 (ITU-R, 2021; editorial amendment 2026)**: complex dielectric framework for water, salt water, soil, ice and vegetation; conventional σ/ε attachment for P.368/P.832; moisture, temperature, freezing and frequency dependence. Ground electrical parameter uncertainties must be propagated into field uncertainty.
* **Millington/Kirke (1949)**: primary theory/measurement dispute over nonhomogeneous smooth land/sea path. A later field demonstration says the BBC method and reciprocal Millington method fit measured results better than Eckersley in one tested route; it explains conductivity recovery through the vertical field distribution but notes route and parameter uncertainty.
* **Aono & Muramatsu (January 1955, Journal of the Radio Research Laboratories, Vol. 2 No. 7)**: field measurements at 1.85, 2.5 and 4 MHz across Tokyo Bay paths with land/sea sections. Their accessible figures compare measurements with Millington and alternatives; they report fill-in, but also conditional model disagreement. The accessible article does not establish a general forecast for other shoreline geometry.
* **P.526-16 (ITU-R, approved 2025)**: terrain diffraction, first Fresnel ellipsoid, spherical-earth diffraction, surface electrical parameter, and knife-edge/multiple-obstacle/general path methods. It recommends LFMF-SmoothEarth for sub-10 MHz spherical-earth diffraction calculations and provides methods from 10 MHz and up. Terrain model is not a surface-wave channel phase oracle.

### Modern measured evidence and contrary findings

* **Adams et al. (NTIA TR-84-151, July 1984)**: measured 3–30 MHz path losses over four Boulder paths to 45 km; actual test frequencies 5.25, 7.5, 11.5 and 28 MHz. It did not directly test 1 or 30 MHz. Measurements were daytime specifically to make groundwave dominant. The receive antenna was mobile on a van, transmitter vertical monopole over a ground screen. Local frequencies/interference and antenna patterns were checked; active receive antenna suffered power-line/external noise so a passive antenna was used. The authors measured ground constants by wave tilt near the transmitter and say those values were less than a factor of 5 from best-fit values.
* In that report, median path loss at the same frequency/range varies substantially across roughness: 28 MHz medians at 5 km were 100 dB on East, 100 dB on East-snow, 103 dB on South and 120 dB on Mountain; at 20 km these were 131, 129, 138 and 140 dB for rows with data. The measurement distributions’ upper–lower decile span at 28 MHz was 3–13 dB among listed path/distance combinations, and path-specific report summary says 13 dB mountainous vs 6 dB smooth. Smooth-earth and WAGNER path-profile predictions differed by up to 15 dB for 28 MHz irregular paths; the latter sometimes matched valley/peak behavior well and sometimes missed measured median by up to 25 dB. The authors caution the skywave effect in measurements was not determined, so “daytime groundwave” still had a residual component uncertainty. This is direct counterevidence to “30 MHz can be represented as one LOS scalar,” while also preventing a claim that WAGNER is universally accurate.
* **Fockens et al. (2022, peer-reviewed IEEE Transactions on EMC)**: recent urban residential measurement (16 locations, 1.8–28 MHz) abstract reports propagation loss higher than ITU groundwave expectation across the band and a different roll-off. Full article access through its repository returned 403, leaving measurement setup, per-frequency values and residuals unverified here. Keep as a warning that urban loss may exceed smooth reference, not a replacement numeric curve.
* **Hehenkamp et al. (2024, Sensors)**: full open article uses an MF R-Mode Baltic testbed and 2020 four-day shipborne dataset. It computes complex attenuation and phase using a modified C++ LFMF implementation plus an integral equation for variable surface impedance. A predicted phase-delay correction generally improved position/range, but they report specific overcompensation where local land conductivity or Baltic salinity differed from atlas values; during evening skywave superimposed the groundwave and strongly distorted range estimates. This supports separate ground phase and skywave paths, but the carrier band is 283.5–325 kHz, below 1 MHz. Quantitative corrections do not transfer to the modem bands.
* **Valčić et al. (12 February 2026, open-access preliminary communication)**: observations at 490 kHz show daytime detection from a nearby sea-path station but failures from farther stations; authors compare homogeneous and Millington predictions with approximate station gains and ground properties. The chosen uniform path approximation causes large differences from mixed-path estimates, while measurement/model fit remains limited by uncertain station and receiver characteristics. Their conclusion is that this particular model is inadequate for that site; treat it as a warning about uncertain inputs/operational chain, not proof that P.368 is invalid in general or direct evidence at 1 MHz.

## Alternatives, computational cost and physical failure

1. **Flat-earth Sommerfeld/Norton** — low cost, closed-form or tabulated attenuation and useful cross-check at short ranges over uniform ground. It misses Earth curvature/horizon and the correct transition for longer paths; use it to test a smooth short-range limit, not for 45–1000 km or sea/land boundaries without specific theory.
2. **Spherical smooth-earth residue-series/LFMF-SmoothEarth** — standards reference for the groundwave field through 30 MHz. Numerical work is heavier than a ray or single coefficient, but offline precomputation plus interpolation is practical for frequency × σ × ε × distance × height grid. This is an arithmetic cost, not expensive full-wave mesh solution; do not recompute every sample. Use a native C++ reusable kernel or lookup in operating path; Python can analyze sources or generate plots, never own the receiver's persistent DSP state.
3. **Millington reciprocal mixed-path** — modest cost; piecewise homogeneous tables and two traversals. Good first extension for coast/soil sections; fails to represent fine boundary shape, stratified coast/underlay, irregular terrain or complex phase fully. Full-wave perturbation integral is supported by Hehenkamp et al. for slowly varying surface impedance but depends on local complex surface maps.
4. **Terrain diffraction / P.526, P.1812, P.1546** — path-profile and probabilistic methods; use for terrain/clutter and coverage where their statistical/service output fits. P.526 smooth spherical Earth is a base, with profile/obstacle methods for diffraction. P.1812-8 is current and supports 30 MHz point-to-area paths with detailed profiles; P.1546 is a point-to-area alternative. They can be less sample-level expensive than a full waveform terrain solver but are not physically interchangeable with field-of-a-vertical-monopole P.368 or complex phased response.
5. **MoM/FEM/SSPE/parabolic equation/full-wave terrain solver** — useful when strong relief, layered medium, shoreline transitions or mixed reflection/diffraction invalidate homogeneous methods. Cost ranges from repeated profile marching to large meshes; depends on resolution, path length and frequency. Recent 490-kHz NAVTEX work tried MoM+split-step PE and FEMPE with approximate terrain and got overly favorable loss predictions, reinforcing need for measured holdout rather than “numerical = more accurate.” At 30 MHz wavelength 10 m, practical geometry/clutter resolution can dominate runtime. Not a current product simulator recommendation until field-discriminating validation justifies the cost.
6. **Measured empirical site model** — fit median/quantiles at known range/frequency/antenna for a particular route or area. Useful for local outages/reception probability and may beat nominal physics model where buildings/soil map are unknown, but it is not transportable to other sites, seasons or path states; it rarely provides coherent delay/phase. Maintain a physical reference alongside it so the empirical fit does not hide wrong antenna calibration or skywave.

1. Add reference field/power/antenna-plane normalization with validation and explicit unknown calibration status.
2. Add a groundwave-only homogeneous reference lookup or native LFMF adapter with σ/ε, frequency, range and antenna height as stated inputs; test at both 1 MHz and 30 MHz with field-level receipt and scalar-link conversion.

6. Add a separate delayed skywave component under HF regime control, not inside the ground attenuation coefficient.
7. Add measured site/RFI/environment distributions and severe paths after a matched antenna-plane data set establishes their joint occurrence. Use broad supported stress cases (very dry/frozen ground, low power/inefficient small receive antenna, terrain path, mixed coast, strong neighboring carrier, absent groundwave), but label synthetic composition as stress rather than measured percentile.

Keep the no-groundwave/erasure outage baseline and a measured physical antenna response. This campaign would check the research recommendations; it has not been run here.

## Executed desk checks (not modem qualification)

The script [groundwave_checks.py](groundwave_checks.py) and captured output [groundwave_checks.txt](groundwave_checks.txt) use only the Python standard library. They make three independent source-backed checks:

1. **Reference normalization**: P.368's 300 mV/m at 1 km converts to 109.54 dBµV/m; its basic-loss formula agrees with free-space 1-km loss to 0.008 dB at both 1 and 30 MHz. It checks formula/unit consistency, not P.368 accuracy at a real site.
2. **Power/voltage normalization**: at fixed impedance, a 20 dB power loss is a 0.1 complex-amplitude ratio and 0.01 power ratio; under fixed receiver noise variance the SNR shifts −20dB. This guards against applying a power ratio as a sample-amplitude ratio.
3. **Ground property frequency contrast**: conduction/displacement ratio for four representative ground pairs at 1 and 30 MHz. The most ordinary dry/wet soil pairs change from order-unity or conduction-dominant at 1MHz to displacement-dominant at 30MHz; average-salinity seawater remains conduction-dominant. This supports the decision to retain both σ and ε and re-evaluate per frequency.
4. **Geometry screen**: k=4/3 radio horizon for low antenna heights and ITU P.526 first Fresnel radius for 1/10/45 km at both frequencies. The screen makes the 30MHz near-ground obstruction scale concrete while emphasizing that neither horizon nor Fresnel radius computes the surface wave or imposes a hard reception range.

No P.368 executable was run, no simulator was authored, and no modem build, waveform/receiver run, propagation campaign or physical measurement was attempted. The official software package is referenced but not fetched/executed in this branch. Therefore this report supports a design choice and a reviewable desk reproduction, not implementation correctness, a site link budget, model qualification or RF performance.
