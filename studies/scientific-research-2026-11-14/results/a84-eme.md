# Passive 5.8 GHz lunar EME channel evidence

**Scope.** Primary/foundational evidence about passive lunar EME around 5.8 GHz: delay/Doppler structure, libration, cross-frequency correlation, and receiver/design consequences. The public-source set does not establish a complete stochastic channel law or cross-carrier correlation measurements. Frequency-specific claims are labeled as measured, modelled, or inferred.

## Findings for the engineering model

### What is common across carriers

Lunar EME is a distributed, two-way scatter path. For a lunar surface point, delay is set by its Earth-to-point plus point-to-Earth range; Doppler is set by the projected velocity of that reflecting point. Pettengill et al. give the pointwise geometry and equations: round-trip delay \(2D/c\), Doppler approximately \(-2\nu\dot D/c\), with \(\dot D\) including lunar-center range rate, parallax, and apparent lunar rotation. Their 1974 mapping work used delay-Doppler observations at 3.8 cm (about 7.9 GHz) and 70 cm (about 428 MHz). This is a physical reason to share *geometry and scatterer coordinates* between carriers; the Doppler in Hz scales with carrier frequency for a given radial velocity. It does not imply equal per-carrier complex gains or equal Doppler spectra after frequency-dependent surface weighting. [Pettengill et al., “The Mapping of Lunar Radar Scattering Characteristics,” §§3–4, Eqs. 13–21 and observations](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/search.jsp?R=19740048201) ([accessible PDF](https://www.wa5vjb.com/Lunarpdfs/Lunar%20Radar%20Scattering.pdf)).

The lunar center’s bulk range-rate Doppler is a largely common, trackable carrier offset. Differential Doppler across the visible disk is a separate, time/location-dependent spreading mechanism caused by apparent lunar rotation (“libration”), including monthly orbital eccentricity/inclination effects and daily Earth-rotation contribution. K1JT’s EME analysis gives maximum limb offset \(f_0=2fR\omega/c\) and full limb-to-limb width \(2f_0=4fR\omega/c\); width scales linearly with carrier and varies substantially with date, observer latitude, and mutual path geometry. Thus a receiver should remove/predict the common center Doppler while preserving the residual distribution; one shared Doppler in Hz for all carriers is physically wrong. A shared underlying angular-rate/geometric field, scaled by each carrier, is grounded. [Taylor, “Frequency-Dependent Characteristics of the EME Path,” §§“Lunar Librations” and “Amateur Libration Measurements”](https://wsjt.sourceforge.io/EME2010_K1JT.pdf).

### 5.8 GHz magnitude and delay profile

The 2010 EME paper’s table gives at **5760 MHz** a calculated self-echo full limb-to-limb Doppler width of **164 Hz maximum / 86 Hz median** (latitude ±40°), and median measured-profile proxies **w10=60 Hz, w50=25 Hz**. w50/w10 are defined from echo-spectrum autocorrelation, approximately widths containing 50%/90% of echo power, respectively. The spread table is not a direct 5.76 GHz width measurement: actual amateur measurements assembled in that paper cover 144 MHz–2.3 GHz; it draws 5.76 GHz values from the geometry and frequency trend/model. The source says median w50/w10 include higher-frequency growth of the active reflecting region and describes 3.4 and 5.76 GHz values as expected. Use these as a challenging-case planning scale, not a calibrated universal channel. With the paper’s heuristic inverse-width timescale, median 1/w10 and 1/w50 are roughly **17 ms and 40 ms**, while full maximum-width scale is about 6 ms; these are rough fading/coherence proxies, not formal coherence-time definitions. Actual two-station width must use both endpoints’ vector libration contributions. [Taylor 2010, Fig. 9/table and §§“Consequences for EME”](https://wsjt.sourceforge.io/EME2010_K1JT.pdf).

For delay, the geometric **maximum excess round-trip delay is 11.6 ms** from near disk center to lunar limb. Most received energy is much more concentrated near the leading edge because the lunar surface is quasi-specular at centimeter wavelengths. Integrating Hagfors’ measured angular reflectivity profile, K1JT reports the first 210 μs contains half of echo power at 1.3 GHz and the first 650 μs at 8 GHz. At 430 MHz that half-power delay is 120 μs. The 5.76 GHz median/quantile is not directly tabulated; 8 GHz is a nearby upper-frequency anchor, not a measured 5.8 GHz channel. The report says increasing frequency enlarges the active reflecting area, giving somewhat more delay/Doppler spread than a constant-radius geometric model. Model a short, power-dominant near-center component plus a weak extended tail toward 11.6 ms; do not force either all energy into the first few hundred microseconds or a uniform 11.6 ms profile. Exact 5.8 GHz power-delay statistics remain an important measurement gap. [Taylor 2010, “Fundamentals of Lunar Radar,” Fig. 2 discussion and Fig. 1 caption](https://wsjt.sourceforge.io/EME2010_K1JT.pdf).

### Reflection is between specular and diffuse

Smooth-surface reflection would be specular with very short delay and low Doppler spread; a uniformly rough/diffuse disk would populate the full delay/Doppler extent and depolarize strongly. Practical EME lies between those limits, becoming more diffuse as frequency increases. Lunar radar literature likewise separates quasi-specular and diffuse components: Hagfors-profiled backscatter is strongest near normal incidence; centimeter-wave diffuse/cross-polarized returns depend on wavelength-scale rocks and subsurface scattering. Therefore frequency changes not only Doppler scaling but also which lunar patches and scattering mechanisms contribute. Avoid assuming one identical, normalized power-delay profile at all carriers. [Taylor 2010, “Consequences for EME”; Campbell et al., “Modeling Radar Scattering from Icy Lunar Regoliths at 13 cm and 4 cm Wavelengths,” §2](https://doi.org/10.1029/2009JE003368); [Hagfors, “Remote Probing of the Moon … by Radar” (1970)](https://doi.org/10.1029/RS005i002p00189).

Empirical primary mapping supports real spatially structured, polarization-sensitive lunar scatter rather than an ideal mirror: Pettengill et al. map delay-Doppler power and both circular-polarization senses at 3.8/70 cm; NASA’s GBT 12.6 cm dual-polarization map archive documents same/opposite-sense maps and a pointwise “patch focusing” correction for time-varying Doppler away from the radar pointing target. Those are monostatic radar datasets, not communications-channel measurements; the 12.6 cm (2.38 GHz) work is adjacent-frequency evidence, while 3.8 cm is the closest foundational high-frequency radar wavelength (7.9 GHz). [NASA NTRS 1974](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/search.jsp?R=19740048201); [NASA PDS, GBT 12.6 cm radar maps](https://pds.nasa.gov/ds-view/pds/viewProfile.jsp?dsid=ARCB%2FNRAO-L-RTLS%2FGBT-5-12.6CM-V1.0).

### Cross-carrier complex gains and practical receiver choices

No examined source reports measured cross-frequency complex channel correlation for simultaneous EME carriers. A reasonable physics model is a shared, slowly changing *surface/geometry process* with carrier-specific scattering coefficients and phase, not a single complex gain copied to all carriers and not independent arbitrary gains re-drawn every sample. For carrier \(f_c\), a distributed model can sum surface patches with delay \(\tau_p(t)\), radial velocity \(v_p(t)\), and frequency-dependent complex weight \(a_p(f_c,t)\). Geometry couples the carriers; material scattering, coherent phase \(2\pi f_c\tau_p\), polarization, and frequency-dependent active footprint distinguish them.

A useful discriminator is carrier spacing versus delay-induced coherence bandwidth: if two carriers are close compared with the inverse of the *power-weighted* delay spread, their fades can be correlated; for spacing well beyond that scale, shared complex gain is not justified even though libration timing/velocity geometry is common. From the source’s half-power delay anchors (0.21 ms at 1.3 GHz; 0.65 ms at 8 GHz), the rough inverse-delay scale is kilohertz, not megahertz. This is only an order-of-magnitude inference: a rigorous coherence bandwidth requires the measured power-delay profile and a stated correlation threshold. In particular, do not infer multi-MHz or widely separated carriers have equal fades from their shared Moon path.

For a small receiver budget, retain each carrier’s own complex channel/equalizer state; share ephemeris, center Doppler prediction, geometry, and timing where signals are synchronized. Allocate pilots/tracking by each carrier’s measured spread/SNR, with update intervals comfortably shorter than the tens-of-milliseconds median 5.76 GHz fade proxy in the K1JT table; include high-spread windows and edge-of-disk tail. Robust coding/time-frequency diversity is supported by K1JT’s discussion of JT65-like synchronization, continuous tone occupancy, and redundancy as mitigation for fade dropouts; at higher frequency tone spacing must exceed the effective spread enough to avoid self-interference. A short burst with pilots that assume a static narrowband flat channel can miss both the extended tail and rapid fade. Measure at least simultaneous per-carrier channel estimates, delay-Doppler spectra, cross-carrier complex correlation versus frequency spacing/time, and behavior across libration geometries before claiming a shared full response model.

A 2015 Chalmers 1296 MHz EME thesis measured echo Doppler spread and found good agreement with theoretical prediction, but itself cautions that repeatability needs further study. It is valuable independent validation of the geometric/libration approach at 1.296 GHz, not evidence that the 5.76 GHz extrapolated widths or cross-frequency complex gains are exact. [Lindgren, “A 1296 MHz Earth–Moon–Earth Communication System,” abstract and ch. 13, pp. 193–205](https://publications.lib.chalmers.se/records/fulltext/217884/217884.pdf).

## Material limits and next discriminator

- Exact 5.8 GHz time-varying complex impulse response, coherence bandwidth, cross-carrier correlation, and representative power-delay quantiles are not in the sources found.
- The most relevant 5.76 GHz figures are modelled/median estimates from amateur EME practice, not a broad controlled experimental campaign. Published measured EME spectra in the 2010 paper stop at 2.3 GHz in the multi-geometry width plot; it separately shows 10.3 GHz example spectra but not a 5.76 GHz measurement series.
- The 1296 MHz thesis is a single-system study; its abstract explicitly calls for repeatability work.
- The 1974 and GBT radar maps are active monostatic radar, with different waveform, polarization, antenna beam and processing. Their spatial scattering physics transfers; their sampled impulse response/statistics should not be equated to a bistatic communications link.
- Best next discriminator: synchronized multi-frequency EME recording across a known geometry, including calibrated Tx/Rx frequency/phase, enough bandwidth and dynamic range to estimate short-delay dominant energy and low-level tail, with pointwise lunar ephemeris. Estimate per-band PDP/Doppler spectrum and cross-frequency complex correlation (report bandwidth/time averaging and confidence intervals); repeat at distinct libration rates and polarization conditions.
