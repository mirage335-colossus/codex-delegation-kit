# HF channel evidence: delay, Doppler, carrier correlation, and receiver choices

**Branch:** a84 HF primary standards and original measurements. **Date accessed:** 2026-11-14. **Scope:** evidence for moving beyond stationary flat or simple two-ray HF models, HF delay/Doppler spread, carrier-to-carrier dependence, and receiver alternatives. This is literature evidence, not a code finding or performance qualification. I did not inspect the excluded implementation or study materials.

## Decision-relevant synthesis

HF skywave should not be collapsed to a single stationary flat fade or deterministic two-ray channel when testing a receiver expected to encounter multimode, near-MUF, equatorial, auroral, or high-latitude conditions. The established baseline (ITU-R F.1487/Watterson) is itself a *time-varying multipath tapped delay line*: each mode/path has its own delay, strength, Doppler shift and Doppler spectrum/spread, with optional O/X magneto-ionic components and additive noise. F.1487 recommends it for 3 kHz and says it may apply up to 12 kHz; above 12 kHz the recommendation explicitly says generalization is difficult and needs more development/validation. It is a useful reference model, not proof of a universal channel law.

Observed regimes vary substantially. The ITU-R BS.1615-3 physical description says one operational F mode can have up to four components (O/X and high-/low-angle rays) near MUF, giving about 0.3–0.6 ms total time dispersion when working-frequency/MUF >0.9; below 0.9 rays can merge/disappear. It warns of up to about 4 ms during equatorial post-sunset or disturbed auroral conditions. Its WSSUS simulation rationale uses stationary statistics per good/moderate/bad case, which remains practical for repeatable tests but suppresses nonstationary regime transitions unless explicitly added.

The strongest high-latitude measurement counterexample to low-spread, few-specular assumptions is Siddle et al./Warrington et al. Radio Science 41 (2006), using prolonged DAMSON observations on Svalbard–Kiruna and Kirkenes–Kiruna paths. At 95% occurrence and peak-to-mean ratio >3.5, reported composite multipath spreads reach about 13 ms and composite Doppler spreads 39.2 Hz (20 MHz Kirkenes–Kiruna, 2004/05), while *effective* multipath spread can be much smaller because it power-weights modal energy. Definitions and threshold matter; do not interchange these numbers with rms delay spread or a universal worst-case envelope. Their abstract and analysis say northerly paths can exceed midlatitude delay and Doppler spread and exhibit broad arrival-angle distributions, so treating each mode as a single plane/specular component can also fail.

Carrier sharing needs a stricter interpretation. Smith et al. (2001) report that during sunrise on one Norwegian path, for a limited 3 kHz-bandwidth data set, Doppler spreads on noncontiguous channels remained well-correlated across about 0.5 MHz. The authors explicitly say applicability to auroral/equatorial disturbances needs further study. This supports a shared *spread parameter* as a candidate simplification for that condition, not shared complex gain, phase, delay profile, or instantaneous response. Conversely, NTIA transauroral correlation work explains why nearby-frequency envelope correlation depends on delay structure: a two-coherent-ray model with differential delay D predicts alternating maxima/minima spaced 1/D and minima-to-maxima at 1/(2D); many independently perturbed/scattered paths wash out this periodicity. Thus common response across carriers cannot be inferred from nominal carrier spacing alone. Measure the quantity the receiver uses.

For receiver design, retain a common coarse acquisition/timing/Doppler framework if justified, but allow per-carrier complex channel estimates, gains, delay taps and residual frequency/phase tracking. A lower-cost intermediate model can share path topology or slow latent ionospheric state while giving each carrier frequency-dependent path delays, losses, magnetoionic weights and residual tap processes. Test it against independent per-carrier channel realizations and a fully shared-response optimistic bound using matched channel histories. Receiver methods suited to frequency-selective time-varying channels include pilot-aided tapped-delay estimation/tracking, adaptive/Kalman channel tracking, per-tone or per-carrier equalization, diversity combining only after measured correlation, and robust interleaving/coding. Receiver complexity may be reduced by local promotion/adaptation (e.g. increase pilot density, equalizer span or tracking rate when estimated delay/Doppler spread rises) instead of permanently provisioning the worst regime.

## Source findings and applicability

1. **ITU-R F.1487-0 (05/2000), “Testing of HF modems with bandwidths of up to about 12 kHz using ionospheric channel simulators,” in force.** [Official ITU page](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-F.1487-0-200005-I/en), [official PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/f/R-REC-F.1487-0-200005-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf). Annex 1 §§1–4 (PDF pp. 2–6): says several E/F, one-/multi-hop paths can span “several milliseconds”; layer height change creates distinct Doppler shifts; turbulence creates per-component Doppler spread/fading. Watterson is a stationary Gaussian-scatter tapped-delay model with a complex random gain per path, potentially two magnetoionic components. It is validated for 3 kHz and “may be applicable” up to 12 kHz; for 12 kHz–1 MHz it reports insufficient channel information/validation and says a general model cannot yet be recommended. Annex 3 §§2–5 (pp. 10–11) gives representative 2-path values: midlatitude quiet 0.5 ms / 0.1 Hz; disturbed 2 ms / 1 Hz; disturbed NVIS 7 ms / 1 Hz; high-latitude quiet 1 ms / 0.5 Hz, moderate 3 ms / 10 Hz, disturbed 7 ms / 30 Hz. It says delay >5 ms and Doppler spread >25 Hz occur about 5% of the time at high latitudes. These are prescribed representative test cases, not universal population statistics. Annex 2 (pp. 6–7) suggests modem performance surfaces over delay, Doppler spread and S/N; examples show an 8-PSK 2400 bit/s modem degrading near 4 Hz and 10 ms. Reusable comparative method, but receiver/mode specifics matter.

2. **ITU-R BS.1615-3 (11/2023), planning parameters for digital broadcasting below 30 MHz.** [Official PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1615-3-202311-I%21%21PDF-E.pdf), Attachment 3 to Annex 1 §§2,4 (PDF pp. 12–14; extracted lines 478–504). Gives ray-delay calculation from P.533 virtual slant range, then the near-MUF O/X and ray conditions above. It describes WSSUS stochastic time-varying models with stationary statistics as the simulation approach, noting real-channel BER results lie between best/worst simulated cases. Caveat: simulation/statistics guidance does not demonstrate that stationarity holds across a changing HF session.

3. **Siddle, Warrington & Stocker, “Measurement and modeling of HF channel directional spread characteristics for northerly paths,” Radio Science 41 (2006), DOI 10.1029/2005RS003294.** [Full text](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2005RS003294), [PDF copy](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=000d345c85dd991a9ec40f4f3b40722100978051&repid=rep1&type=pdf). Abstract and §3, Table 3: long measurement campaign on two high-latitude paths; reports 95% occurrence summary by frequency/year/path. Composite spread is overall temporal extent without relative modal power; effective multipath spread accounts for modal powers and had predicted modem tests better. Examples: 4.5 MHz Svalbard–Kiruna 2003 composite 7.7 ms/effective 0.3 ms/Doppler 1 Hz; 5.8 MHz same path 2003 12.0/1.5 ms/2 Hz; 20 MHz Kirkenes–Kiruna 2005 5.3 ms composite, 0.4 ms effective, 39.2 Hz Doppler. 14.4 MHz Kirkenes–Kiruna 2003 reports 6.7 ms composite, 1.0 ms effective and 29.8 Hz Doppler. Their 80%-power azimuth/elevation spread definition uses >−15 dB peak threshold; Table 3 also conditions on peak-to-mean power ratio >3.5. High values are real but path-, frequency-, year-, threshold- and metric-specific, not a blanket operating distribution.

4. **Watterson, Juroshek & Bensema, “Experimental confirmation of an HF channel model,” IEEE Trans. Communication Technology 18(6), 792–803 (1970), DOI 10.1109/TCOM.1970.1090438.** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TCOM.1970.1090438). Abstract record describes specially designed measurements validating independent complex Gaussian/Rayleigh tap gains and Gaussian Doppler spectra for daytime and nighttime, with practical bandwidth limited to about one fourth reciprocal of effective weighted modal time spread. The historical evidence supports the model in its sampled domain; NTIA Report 90-255 notes original validation used 36 minutes on a single ~800 km path and reported 2.5, 8 and 12 kHz bandwidths during three periods of one day. Do not extrapolate to wideband, other paths, high latitudes, or nonstationary changes without measurement.

5. **NTIA transauroral fading-correlation report (original measurement).** [GovInfo PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-C13-152cf80d492665dd7ee599c97d850018/pdf/GOVPUB-C13-152cf80d492665dd7ee599c97d850018.pdf), §§3.1–3.2, pp. 5–7 (PDF lines 101–126). Distinguishes slow absorption fading (similar across normal HF bandwidth) from faster frequency-selective multipath fading. A coherent two-path model predicts correlation oscillation tied to differential delay; many paths/scatter and independent ionospheric perturbations erase periodicity. Measurements used AM carrier and sidebands with varying audio offsets; limited simultaneous spacings due logistics/SNR. It is an old, path-specific envelope-correlation study, useful for mechanism, not a universal numerical correlation law.

6. **Smith et al., “Simultaneous measurements of propagation characteristics on noncontiguous HF channels,” IEE Antennas and Propagation 2001, DOI 10.1049/cp:20010311.** [Institutional record/abstract](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/simultaneous-measurements-of-propagation-characteristics-on-nonco/). One Norway path, initial limited 3 kHz data focused on sunrise; Doppler spreads correlated across roughly 0.5 MHz. Authors explicitly call for more evidence in auroral/equatorial disturbances. Abstract-only/full proceedings not inspected here. The observed shared statistic is spread, not complex response.

7. **DRDC-RDDC-2018-D122, “Sounding principles for high latitude BLOS HF channels.”** [Government report PDF](https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2019/rddc-drdc/D68-2-122-2018-eng.pdf), §3.1, pp. 7–8. Defines sampled time-varying impulse response h(n,l); explains that even common 3/12 kHz “narrowband” communications can be frequency-selective when multimode delay exceeds a symbol interval. It warns Watterson single-mode default is flat and cannot model spread-F (within-F-layer time dispersion), and that its foundation was short measurement campaign on one midlatitude path; wider bandwidth makes per-mode dispersion more relevant. Discusses sounding/training sequence tradeoffs under rapidly time-varying high-latitude channels and cautions coherence-time assumptions (§5). Useful measurement/estimation design reference; report not a new channel survey.

8. **Basler, “Ionospheric distortion of HF signals,” Radio Science 23(4), 569–579 (1988), DOI 10.1029/RS023i004p00569.** [Abstract and DOI](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1029/RS023i004p00569). Disturbed polar-cap, auroral and equatorial one-hop F experiments used 20 kHz PN phase modulation; observed range/propagation-time distortion on order hundreds of microseconds and Doppler-domain distortions on order tens of hertz. Supports non-flat doubly dispersive conditions; abstract-level figures only, precise distributions/conditions require full paper.

## Practical alternative families and discriminating check

- **Repeatable baseline:** F.1487 two independently fading modes with equal power, controlled delay and Gaussian Doppler spectra; sweep its low-, mid-, high-latitude parameters. Useful for controlled comparative BER/performance surfaces, but by design not the hardest channel.
- **Richer single-carrier baseline:** power-weighted multimode tapped delay line, per-tap two-component O/X Doppler spectra/offsets, optional stable specular component, and time variation of taps or regime transitions. Calibrate with route-, hour-, season- and disturbance-specific soundings. Include diffuse spread-F/scatter (not just discrete taps), and angular spread if array processing matters.
- **Three-carrier candidate hierarchy:** (a) independent per-carrier CIR/gains (conservative independence bound); (b) shared slow latent path-state and correlated Doppler-spread process but carrier-specific complex taps and frequency-dependent delay/attenuation; (c) fully common tap trajectories (optimistic strong-correlation bound). Use identical measured or synthesized environment histories across candidate receivers. Report correlation of complex CIR, per-tap envelopes/phases, tap delays and spread parameters separately. Compare fused/shared receiver throughput with per-carrier oracle/tracker baselines and equal resource budgets. Three simultaneous, synchronized carriers are necessary to determine whether apparent parameter correlation is real in the target regime; sequential frequency retuning confounds time evolution with frequency dependence.
- **Simpler robust receiver:** estimate per-carrier channel on pilots, do adaptive time/frequency equalization and soft combining; permit a common coarse Doppler center only after checking observed inter-carrier covariance. Use measured diversity correlation before claiming gain. Adjust pilots/equalizer span/decoder protection based on observed spread. This keeps hard processing capacity for difficult cases without assuming a shared channel.

**Next discriminator:** acquire synchronized sounding on the actual three carriers, recording complex frequency response/impulse response and SNR over the same intervals, with enough probing bandwidth and cadence for expected delay/Doppler spreads. Stratify quiet/moderate/disturbed and sunrise/sunset, path and MUF ratio. Compare (i) cross-carrier complex CIR correlation/coherence and per-tap correlation, (ii) delay and Doppler spread correlations, and (iii) held-out receiver throughput using shared-state, shared-response and independent-response estimators. Match power, pilot overhead, noise/interference, frequency spacing, antenna/polarization and true time alignment. This resolves the presently unsupported jump from correlated Doppler-spread metrics to a shared channel response.
