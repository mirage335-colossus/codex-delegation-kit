## Does decision-focused language inherently prevent exploration?

No. “Decisions” can include discovering the available options, interpreting new
observations and deciding which questions deserve investigation. Existing text
explicitly asks for unfamiliar mechanisms and alternative approaches before
convergence. A competent execution could satisfy the user without another edit.

There is nevertheless a plausible operational ambiguity. The assignment cue,
source-to-decision chain, follow-up justification and stopping language repeatedly
ask what changes a development decision. An owner can interpret relevance as
impact on its *current* plan. Under that interpretation, an unrecognized
mechanism has to demonstrate its usefulness before the reading needed to
recognize it occurs. Research becomes a search for missing evidence inside an
already selected question set. Separate readiness labels do not necessarily
change that selection rule.

The smallest different approach is to make explanatory discovery an explicit
part of the existing research responsibility. It should expose relationships,
assumptions, alternative formulations and transfer limits across the actual
problem. Reading may refine the question map and reveal equivalent or additional
approaches before a specific implementation consequence is known. The resulting
understanding then supports choices and executed discriminators. This broadens
the reason to investigate, while preserving the engineering goal and finite
scope; it does not reward novelty or additional algorithms for their own sake.
